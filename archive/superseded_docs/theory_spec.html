Superseded — kept for provenance

This is the first specification draft and it is wrong in at least one load-bearing place. It appeals to Hammersley–Clifford to establish that the single-position conditionals determine a unique joint. That argument does not apply here: Hammersley–Clifford needs the support to be a product of coordinate supports, and 𝒮(𝒦) is not — it forbids repeats and fixes category counts. The current specification proves the same result by Brook's telescoping argument over swap-connectedness, which needs no positivity condition.

It also predates the persistence terms, the habit loading, the third penalty block, the truncated breadth, the n ≥ 1 conditioning, and the removal of negative sampling. Every number and every equation here should be read as historical.

The current specification is paper/model_spec.html, and the results that accompany it are in paper/experiments.html.

Specification

The nested basket model

Part I defines the random variables, by giving the process that generates them and the noise in each step. Part II states the distribution that process induces, one factor at a time. Part III derives the two results Part II depends on. Part IV is estimation. Reference material is in the appendices.

Part IThe random variables

1What is given

Everything below is conditional on a trip descriptor, observed and never modelled:

𝒢 = ( i,   s,   t,   w,   {Δlog pjst}j=1..J )
(1)

household i (N = 2,066), store s (115), day t (D = 712), week-of-year w (52), and a price deviation per item. Also fixed: J = 5,455 items; C = 188 categories; c(j) the category of item j; and 𝒞s(c) the items of category c that store s stocks (median 15, max 225). Price and assortment construction is Appendix B.

2The generating process

A basket is produced in four steps. Each step introduces one latent variable with an explicit error distribution, and each observed variable is a function of those latents. The systematic terms η, zb, u−j and z are defined in §3; read them as known numbers for now.

1

Which categories. Each category draws a threshold shock εc, independently across the C categories, from the minimum-form Gumbel with CDF F(x) = 1 − exp(−ex). The category is entered when the shock falls below the category's systematic appeal:

εc ~ Gumbelmin(0, 1),     yc = 1{ εc ≤ ηict } ∈ {0, 1}
(2)
2

How many products from each. For every entered category, a count of additional products beyond the first:

mc ~ Poisson( ezbict ),     kc = 1 + mc ∈ {1, …, |𝒞s(c)|}
(3)

Steps 1 and 2 together fix the composition and the basket size:

𝒦 = { (c, kc) : yc = 1 },     n = ∑(c,kc) ∈ 𝒦 kc
(4)
3

Which products. The composition creates n positions, kc of them assigned to category c. Unlike the other three, this step is not a recipe you can run. It states a property that S must satisfy, and §9 shows exactly one distribution has that property.

The property is this. Suppose S occupies the n positions. Take the position held by j. Its admissible products are its category's stock, less whatever occupies the other positions:

A−j = 𝒞s(c(j)) \ ( S \ {j} )
(5)

Each admissible product draws a taste shock and the position holds the best of them:

j = argmaxm ∈ A−j [ u−jm + εm ],    εm ~ Gumbel(0, 1) iid over A−j
(6)

Eq. 6 must hold at every position simultaneously, with the other n−1 held fixed. It is a condition on S, not a construction of it: both A−j and u−jm depend on the other occupants, so there is no order in which the positions could be filled one at a time.

Why this is not the circularity that invalidates a model. A set of conditional laws need not correspond to any joint distribution, and a factorisation that cannot be ordered defines nothing. Neither problem arises here, because P(S | 𝒦) is defined on whole baskets at once — Eq. 19, a function of S alone, with no reference to a filling order. Eq. 6 is then a theorem about that distribution (§9), not its definition. The distinction is the difference between "S is whatever this loop produces", which would be circular, and "S has this law, and here is a property it satisfies", which is not.

4

How many units of each. For every product in S, a count of additional units beyond the first:

rj ~ Poisson( ezijt ),     qj = 1 + rj ∈ {1, 2, …}
(7)

The observed basket is the tuple

ℬ = ( y1..C,   {kc}c : yc=1,   S,   {qj}j ∈ S )
(8)

and S is constrained by construction to match the composition:

S ∈ 𝒮(𝒦) := { S′ ⊆ ⋃c𝒞s(c) : |S′ ∩ 𝒞s(c)| = kc ∀ (c, kc) ∈ 𝒦,   S′ ∩ 𝒞s(c) = ∅ ∀ c : yc = 0 }
(9)

3The systematic terms

Functions of 𝒢 and the parameters, indexing the distributions of §2. Parameters and shapes are in Appendix A.

Product appeal, ignoring the basket.

bijt = λj + θi⊤αj − (γi⊤βj) Δlog pjst + ηj⊤xijt + μj⊤δw + ζj⊤ξs
(10)

Product appeal in a given position. A position is identified by whose seat it is: write −j for the position currently held by j, and ᾱ−j for the mean of α over its n−1 neighbours. The appeal of any candidate m in that position is

ᾱ−j = 1n−1 ∑k ∈ S, k ≠ j αk  (:= 0 if n = 1),     u−jm = bimt + αm⊤ᾱ−j
(11)

Two indices, not one. The superscript is the position and the subscript is the candidate, and they differ: u−jm is what m would be worth in j's seat. The incumbent's own value is u−jj. Every candidate for a position is scored against the same ᾱ−j, because the other n−1 occupants are what they are; the context belongs to the seat, not the occupant. §9 shows this is forced rather than conventional.

Writing a single uijt would suppress the position index and make the quantity look like a property of the product alone. It is not: the same product has a different value in a different seat of the same basket.

Category appeal — the quantity in Eq. 2, via the inclusive value:

IVict = log ∑j ∈ 𝒞s(c) ebijt
(12)
ηict = c0c + cu⊤iccc + cs⊤cxict + κc( IVict − IVc )
(13)

IV is built from b, not u: step 1 runs before any product exists, so no basket context is available to it.

Count rates — the quantities in Eqs. 3 and 7:

zbict = b0c + bui − bpc Δlog pct      zijt = q0j − (γq⊤iβqj) Δlog pjst + ηq⊤jxijt
(14)

xijt, xict are four-vectors of purchase recency (Appendix B.3). Δlog pct is the mean of Δlog p over 𝒞s(c). IVc is a constant fixed during fitting (§13.3). κc = softplus(κrawc) > 0.

Part IIThe induced distribution

4The joint

The four steps of §2 give four factors, each conditioning only on what precedes it:

P(ℬ | 𝒢) = ∏c=1C P(yc) · ∏c : yc=1 P(kc) · P(S | 𝒦) · ∏j ∈ S P(qj)
(15)

5P(yc), from step 1

By Eq. 2, yc = 1 exactly when εc ≤ ηict, so the probability is the Gumbelmin CDF evaluated at η:

P(yc = 1 | 𝒢) = F(ηict) = 1 − exp( −eηict )
(16)

A complementary log-log link, independent across the C categories.

6P(kc), from step 2

P(kc = κ | yc = 1, 𝒢) = e−λbc (λbc)κ−1 (κ − 1)!,    λbc = ezbict,    κ = 1, 2, …
(17)

Truncated above at |𝒞s(c)|; independent across entered categories.

7P(S | 𝒦), from step 3

Eq. 6 gives the law of one position given the others. By McFadden's theorem, iid Gumbel errors over Aj make the argmax multinomial-logit:

P( j | S \ {j}, 𝒦 ) = exp( bijt + αj⊤ᾱ−j ) ∑m ∈ A−j exp( bimt + αm⊤ᾱ−j )
(18)

These n conditionals determine a joint over S only if they are mutually compatible, which is not automatic — each depends on the others through ᾱ. §9 proves they are, and that the unique joint they define is

P(S | 𝒦, 𝒢) = exp E(S) ∑S′ ∈ 𝒮(𝒦) exp E(S′),     E(S) = ∑j ∈ S bijt + 1n−1 ∑{j,k} ⊂ S αj⊤αk
(19)

Unlike the other three factors this does not decompose over items — the products in a basket are dependent, which is the purpose of the pairwise term.

Part IIIThe two results Part II rests on

9Why the conditionals cohere, and what that costs

Take S ∈ 𝒮(𝒦), a product j ∈ S in category c, and a replacement m ∈ 𝒞s(c) \ S. Put S′ = (S \ {j}) ∪ {m}. Category counts are unchanged so S′ ∈ 𝒮(𝒦), and |S′| = n so the factor 1/(n−1) is common to both. In E(S′) − E(S) every b except bj, bm cancels, as does every pair involving neither. What survives:

E(S′) − E(S) = (bm − bj) + αm⊤ᾱ−j − αj⊤ᾱ−j = u−jm − u−jj
(21)

The final step holds because m's context in S′ and j's context in S are the same vector ᾱ−j — both are the mean of α over S \ {j}. Exponentiating and normalising over A−j recovers Eq. 18 exactly. So the Gibbs measure Eq. 19 has precisely the conditionals that step 3 generates.

Since exp E(S) > 0 on all of 𝒮(𝒦), the positivity condition of Hammersley–Clifford holds and Brook's lemma makes that joint unique. Hence Eq. 19 is the distribution step 3 defines, not merely a distribution consistent with it.

What it costs. Eq. 21 needs the pairwise term to be symmetric. Were the interaction ρj⊤αk with ρ a free second embedding, then ρj⊤αk ≠ ρk⊤αj, no potential function would exist, the conditionals of step 3 would be incompatible, and P(S | 𝒦) would not exist at all. This is why the interaction reuses α instead of a separate parameter.

What this does not claim: the basket is not one Gumbel argmax over 𝒮(𝒦). A Markov random field has no single-shot random-utility representation. Errors are drawn per position per visit, which is why §10 samples by a Markov chain rather than in closed form.

10How S is drawn

Eq. 19 cannot be evaluated directly. The number of baskets matching a composition is

|𝒮(𝒦)| = ∏(c,kc) ∈ 𝒦 |𝒞s(c)|! kc! (|𝒞s(c)| − kc)!
(22)

measured on 3,000 held-out compositions at median 106.7, 90th percentile 1028.6, maximum 10124. Neither E(S) nor the normaliser is ever computed. Eq. 21 makes that unnecessary: only ratios between baskets differing in one position are needed, and those are Eq. 18, a sum over at most 225 terms.

  1. For each (c, kc) ∈ 𝒦, draw kc distinct products from 𝒞s(c) in proportion to eb, without replacement. Any member of 𝒮(𝒦) is a valid start.
  2. Visit the n positions in random order. At position j: recompute ᾱ−j from the current S, form A−j by Eq. 5, draw a replacement from Eq. 18, update S.
  3. Repeat step 2 for a fixed number of sweeps; return S.

Every move preserves each category's count, so S never leaves 𝒮(𝒦); any member is reachable from any other by successive swaps, so the chain is irreducible on exactly the support of Eq. 19 and converges to it. One sweep costs n conditionals — measured at 8.44 positions × 42 admissible products ≈ 359 evaluations of Eq. 10.

11What is assumed rather than derived

Steps 1 and 3 follow from stated error distributions. Steps 2 and 4 do not: the count is the primitive, with no latent utility beneath it. Three consequences, none currently tested.

  • Equidispersion. A Poisson ties variance to mean. Basket counts are typically overdispersed, which would appear as a fitted rate matching the mean while understating the spread.
  • Units independent given S. Eq. 20 makes qj independent across products, so buying six yoghurts cannot change the units of anything else. All modelled dependence is in which products appear, none in how many.
  • Not the same object as §12's construction. If a latent per-item count Qij were Poisson, kc = #{j : Qij ≥ 1} would be Poisson-binomial, not shifted Poisson, and qj would be zero-truncated Poisson, not 1 + Poisson. Eqs. 17 and 20 share a functional form with that construction, not a derivation.

12The scale of κ

Eq. 13 contains κc without saying what value is neutral. One construction fixes it. Suppose the total units from category c were Q ~ Poisson(eaic+κcIV), split across the category in proportion to eb. A Poisson total split by fixed proportions gives independent Poisson counts per product, with rates ea+κIV·eb−IV, since product j's share is eb−IV by Eq. 12:

Qij ~ Poisson( eaic + (κc−1) IVict + bijt )
(23)

At κc = 1 the inclusive value cancels: each product's rate depends only on its own appeal. Two readings follow, and both are used.

ValueMeaning for a price cut in category c
κc = 0entry ignores the products: share moves within the category and nothing else — pure cannibalisation
κc = 1neutral — the category grows exactly as much as its products gained
κc > 1the category grows more than its products gained — the promotion pulls in trips

κ is therefore initialised at exactly 1.0, so the data must move it off neutral. Note also that yc = 1{Qc ≥ 1} gives P = 1 − e−rate, the same cloglog as Eq. 16 — the two routes to step 1 agree. Eq. 23 is never evaluated; it fixes an interpretation, nothing more.

Part IVEstimation

13Objective

Eqs. 16, 17 and 20 contribute their log-densities directly. Eq. 19 cannot: its normaliser is the intractable sum of Eq. 22. Replace it with the pseudo-likelihood, the sum of one-position conditionals — each of which is Eq. 18 and each of which is exact:

ℓitem(S) = ∑j ∈ S log P( j | S \ {j}, 𝒦 )
(24)
ℒ = ℓitem + wqℓqty + wcℓinc + wbℓbrd − 1M ( ℓ2‖Θrepr‖² + ℓp2‖Θprice‖² )
(25)

Pseudo-likelihood is consistent for this family under standard conditions; it is not efficient and yields no held-out joint score for S. Its gradient differentiates through ᾱ−j, so αj receives gradient both as the scored product and through every other position's context.

Θrepr = {α, θ, η, μ, δ, ζ, ξ, cu, cc, cs}; Θprice = {γ, β, γq, βq}. Penalised separately because shrinking the price block at the representation block's rate biases the elasticity toward zero, and the elasticity is the estimand. M is a fixed constant, so the penalty does not vary with batch composition.

13.1IV is estimated, not summed

Eq. 12 is a log-sum-exp over a category's stock. Sample m of nc uniformly with replacement:

IV = log( ∑r=1..m ebjr ) + log ncm
(26)

The sum is unbiased, 𝔼[(nc/m)∑reb] = ∑jeb; its logarithm is biased low by Jensen. The bias is absorbed into c0c, and generation uses the same estimator so the two stay consistent — valid to the extent the bias is constant within a category, which requires m and the within-category spread of b to be stable. A condition to test, not assume.

13.2Category sampling

Eq. 16 is a Bernoulli over 188 categories per trip, of which 6.12 are entered. Categories are sampled uniformly, without replacement. Case-control sampling biases the fit two ways at a 3.25% base rate: the intercept, which the standard log(π1/π0) offset corrects, and the term κc(IV − IVc), whose mean differs between sample and population because entered categories have higher inclusive values — which the offset does not touch. Uniform sampling removes both at source.

13.3The frozen reference

IVc is fixed early in fitting to the mean IV over a uniform category sample. Any fixed constant is absorbed by c0c, so freezing does not change the fit; it guarantees training and generation subtract the same thing.

13.4Model selection

All four terms of Eq. 25 have held-out counterparts and all four enter the checkpoint criterion. Because Eq. 18 is exact, the item term is a true held-out conditional likelihood, independent of any sampling scheme and comparable across model variants.

BConstruction of the given quantities

B.1Δlog p

Price. The loyalty price a card holder faces at the shelf: (SALES_VALUE − COUPON_MATCH_DISC) / QUANTITY. The coupon match is subtracted because it is a manufacturer refund, not a price the shopper responded to. The paid price is not used: two shoppers at one shelf with different coupons would otherwise face different prices for the same product on the same day.

Per product-day. The median over that day's purchase lines — median, not mean, because one mis-keyed quantity gives a wild per-unit value. 24.7% of the J × D grid is observed; missing days carry the last observed price forward, and days before a product's first sale carry the first observed price backward.

Centring. With 𝒯 the training days, Δlog pjt = log pjt − |𝒯|−1∑t′∈𝒯 log pjt′, where log p = log max(price, 10−3). Every time-aggregate the model uses is formed on 𝒯 alone. Contemporaneous quantities are not restricted.

Store deviation. djsw = log p̃jsw − log p̃jw, store median against chain median that week, kept where |d| > 10−6 — 244,880 cells, 0.53% of the grid. Elsewhere zero, falling back to the chain price. The model reads Δlog pjst = Δlog pjt + djsw.

B.2𝒞s(c)

Product j belongs to 𝒞s(c(j)) if store s sold it at any point in the training window; the threshold is one sale. Covers 50.9% of the product × store grid. There is no stock-out feed, so this is a proxy for assortment, not daily availability.

B.3xijt and xict

With τ the days since household i last bought product j's sub-commodity, by a strictly-before lookup:

xijt = [ 1{never},   e−τ/7,   e−τ/gsub(j),   log(1+τ)/log 100 ]⊤
(27)

with the last three zero when there is no earlier purchase. The three decays express bought it yesterday, about due and long gone separately; gsub(j) is that sub-commodity's median repurchase gap over 𝒯, so "due" means a week for milk and months for shampoo. xict is the same four functions of the days since the household last bought anything in category c.

CPrice derivatives

One margin per factor of Eq. 15. With πj the within-category share:

∂ log πj∂ log pj = −(γi⊤βj)(1 − πj)     κc∂ IV∂ log pj = −κc(γi⊤βj)πj     ∂ log 𝔼[qj]∂ log pj = −(γq⊤iβqj) λj1 + λj
(28)

The first two are exact derivatives of the same normalised score, which is why they carry (1 − πj) and πj and combine to −(γ⊤β)[1 − πj(1 − κc)]. Since 𝔼[qj] = 1 + λj, the units elasticity carries λ/(1+λ) — the floor at one unit damps it.
