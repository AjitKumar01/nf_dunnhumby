\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We build a model of grocery baskets that can answer price counterfactuals and generate
synthetic shopping data. The document is organised around one discipline: every design
choice is justified by a measurement on the data, and every claim about the model is a
number from a held-out test set. We first describe what the data actually contains
(Section~\ref{sec:eda}), then the model (Section~\ref{sec:model}), then a explicit map
from each data finding to the model component that answers it
(Section~\ref{sec:mapping}), then experiments (Section~\ref{sec:exp}). Section~\ref{sec:fail}
lists what the model still gets wrong. The headline results: the model beats the
strongest simple baseline by $+0.342$ nats, its price coefficient survives a
structural placebo (retaining 0.0\%), and synthetic baskets it generates carry
87\% of the real price elasticity. The main remaining failure is that generated
baskets contain roughly the right \emph{amount} of co-purchase structure but put it on
the wrong \emph{pairs}.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}

A retailer wants to know: if I cut the price of one product, what happens? Not just to
that product, but to the whole basket. Do people buy more of it, switch to it from a
competitor, or start buying the category at all when they otherwise would not? And can
we answer this for one household, so that coupons can be targeted?

Answering this needs a model of the whole basket. Three quantities matter and they are
different from each other:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Incidence.} Does this household buy from this category on this trip?
\item \textbf{Allocation.} Given that it does, which product does it pick?
\item \textbf{Quantity.} How many units, and how many different products from the category?
\end{itemize}

A model that only answers ``which product'' cannot distinguish a price cut that grows a
category from one that merely moves share within it. That distinction is the retailer's
actual question.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The dunnhumby ``Complete Journey'' data: two years of transactions from a grocery chain.
After filtering, the working dataset is:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
quantity & value \\
\midrule
households $N$              & 2,066 \\
items $J$                   & 5,455 \\
categories $C$              & 188 \\
sub-commodities $S$         & 758 \\
stores                      & 115 \\
days $D$                    & 712 \\
(basket, item) rows         & 1,566,063 \\
baskets                     & 199,345 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The only item filter is at least 100 purchase lines, which is statistical support
for that item's own parameters. Households need 20--300 trips. Nothing is
dropped to protect a modelling assumption.

\paragraph{Splits are by calendar week, not at random.}
Train is week $<83$, validation 83--90, test $\ge91$. A model whose
point is to answer ``what happens next week'' must be scored on later weeks. A random
split would let it see each household's future.

\paragraph{Prices are deviations, not levels.}
For item $j$ on day $t$ we use
\begin{equation}
\Delta\log p_{jt} \;=\; \log p_{jt} \;-\; \frac{1}{D}\sum_{t'=1}^{D}\log p_{jt'},
\end{equation}
so an item's price \emph{level} is absorbed by its own intercept and what identifies the
response is that item moving against its own normal price. Where a store-level price was
observed we add the store deviation on top; otherwise it falls back to the chain price.

\section{What the data says}
\label{sec:eda}

This section reports measurements taken \emph{before} any model, using nothing but
counts and least squares. Each subsection ends with the design consequence.

\subsection{Demand responds to price}

Build a panel with one row per (item, week). Let $b_{jw}$ be the number of purchase
lines and $T_w$ the number of baskets chain-wide that week. Define
\begin{equation}
y_{jw} \;=\; \log\frac{b_{jw}+0.5}{T_w},
\qquad
x_{jw} \;=\; \text{mean } \log p_{jw} .
\end{equation}
The $0.5$ keeps zero-purchase weeks in the panel. The elasticity is an ordinary least
squares slope after removing each item's own mean from both sides --- a
\emph{within-item} regression, so the comparison is an item against itself over time
rather than expensive items against cheap ones:
\begin{equation}
\hat\varepsilon
\;=\;
\frac{\sum_{j,w}\bigl(x_{jw}-\bar x_j\bigr)\bigl(y_{jw}-\bar y_j\bigr)}
     {\sum_{j,w}\bigl(x_{jw}-\bar x_j\bigr)^2}
\qquad\text{over 556,410 item-weeks.}
\label{eq:within}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
left-hand side & elasticity & what it measures \\
\midrule
log buyers per trip   & $-0.792$  & whether a household buys the item at all \\
log units per trip    & $-0.945$  & total units moved \\
log units per buyer   & $-0.235$  & how much a buyer takes, given that they buy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A 10\% price cut raises total units by about 9.5\%. Of that, $-0.235/-0.945 = 0.25$
comes from existing buyers taking \emph{more}, and the rest from more
households buying at all.

\textbf{Consequence.} A model that treats purchase as binary discards a quarter of the
price response. Quantity needs its own component.

\subsection{A price cut moves three separate margins}

The units bought from a category factor exactly:
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\frac{\text{units}}{\text{basket}}}_{\text{total}}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\text{visits}}{\text{baskets}}}_{\text{incidence}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\text{lines}}{\text{visit}}}_{\text{breadth}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\text{units}}{\text{line}}}_{\text{depth}} .
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
Taking logs, the three elasticities must add to the total. Estimating each with
Eq.~\ref{eq:within} on a (category, week) panel of 15,309 cells, against the mean
price of \emph{every} item in the category that week:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
margin & quantity regressed & elasticity \\
\midrule
incidence & $\log(\text{visits}/\text{baskets})$ & $-0.675$ \\
breadth   & $\log(\text{lines}/\text{visit})$    & $-0.092$ \\
depth     & $\log(\text{units}/\text{line})$     & $-0.158$ \\
\midrule
sum       &                                      & $-0.925$ \\
independent estimate (Eq.~\ref{eq:within}, item panel) & & $-0.945$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The sum matches the independently measured total to 0.021. Two panels, two units of
analysis, no shared code path. About 73\% of the entire units response is
households deciding to enter a category at all.

\textbf{Consequence.} Incidence is the dominant margin and must be modelled explicitly.

\subsection{Promotions: an event study}

Find every item-week where the log price fell by at least $0.15$ against the previous
week. There are 37,132 such events over 4,712 items. Normalise demand by the
item's own mean so a staple and a niche item can be averaged together:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{norm}_{jw} \;=\; b_{jw}\;/\;\overline{b_j}.
\end{equation}

Events are not independent: an item is cut about eight times, and 57.7\% of
consecutive cuts for the same item fall less than seven weeks apart, so their windows
overlap. We therefore also report \textbf{isolated} events --- no other cut of that item
within $\pm3$ weeks --- of which there are 20,725.

Neither $1.0$ nor the pre-period is a valid baseline. $\mathrm{norm}$ averages exactly
$1.0$ over all of an item's weeks \emph{including its own promotions}, and the
pre-period is selected to be expensive by the event definition. We use a \textbf{quiet
week}: an item-week more than three weeks from any cut of that item. There are
368,173, and they run at 0.896 of the item's mean demand at essentially its
normal price.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
weeks from cut & demand & price (log dev.) & demand $\div$ quiet \\
\midrule
$-3$ & 0.925 & $+0.050$ & 1.03 \\
$-2$ & 0.859 & $+0.098$ & 0.96 \\
$-1$ & 0.870 & $+0.123$ & 0.97 \\
$\ \ 0$ & \textbf{2.136} & $-0.194$ & \textbf{2.38} \\
$+1$ & 1.337 & $-0.139$ & 1.49 \\
$+2$ & 1.117 & $-0.055$ & 1.25 \\
$+3$ & 1.065 & $-0.003$ & 1.19 \\
\midrule
quiet week & 0.896 & $+0.011$ & 1.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three readings.

\emph{The pre-period does not ramp.} Demand before the cut is within 3\% of the quiet
level. If retailers timed promotions to demand that was already rising, the spike would
be partly its own cause. It is not.

\emph{The promotion week is far steeper than elasticity alone.} From $-1$ to $0$ the
price moves $-0.317$ in logs and demand moves $\log(2.136/0.870)=+0.898$, an implied
slope of $-2.83$ --- $3.4\times$ the $-0.792$ measured on ordinary week-to-week
variation. A promoted price arrives with an endcap and a shelf tag that the price
variable does not carry.

\emph{Most of the tail is the promotion still running.} At $+1$ the price is still
$0.139$ below normal. By $+3$ the price has returned and demand is 1.19 of quiet.
No offset dips below quiet, so no stockpiling is visible within three weeks.

\subsection{Households differ, and the difference is measurable}

\paragraph{Taste.} Split each household's purchases in half by date. Count purchases per
category in each half, giving two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{188}$, and scale each to unit
length. Compare with a dot product (a cosine, since both are unit length). Averaging
over 2,066 households and comparing against \emph{every} cross-household pair:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
 & cosine \\
\midrule
a household's own two halves & \textbf{0.7838} \\
two different households, all 4,266,290 pairs & 0.4710 \\
ratio & \textbf{1.66$\times$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A stranger already scores 0.4710 because everyone buys milk and bread; the
\emph{gap} is what is personal.

\paragraph{Price sensitivity.} For each household, regress $\log(\text{units})$ on
$\log(\text{price})$ using only (household, item) pairs bought three or more times, after
subtracting each pair's own means:
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta_i \;=\;
\frac{\sum_{j,t} \tilde x_{ijt}\,\tilde y_{ijt}}{\sum_{j,t}\tilde x_{ijt}^{2}},
\qquad
\tilde x_{ijt} = \log p_{jt} - \overline{\log p_{ij}},\quad
\tilde y_{ijt} = \log u_{ijt} - \overline{\log u_{ij}} .
\end{equation}
Across 1,640 households the median is $-0.169$ with p10--p90 of
$-0.427$--$-0.016$. But the spread alone proves nothing: noisy estimates are
spread out even when the truth is identical for everyone. Measuring each household twice
on disjoint halves gives a split-half correlation of only \textbf{$+0.236$} on
1,374 households.

Why so low: the estimator divides by $\sum \tilde x^2$, the price movement that household
actually saw. 18.6\% of qualifying rows come from a (household, item) pair whose
price never moved. Splitting households by how much price movement they had:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & spread of slopes (sd) & share positive \\
\midrule
little price movement & 0.226 & 11.5\% \\
much price movement   & 0.129 & 3.5\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Where prices genuinely moved the estimates are tighter and almost all negative. Much of
the apparent heterogeneity is bad measurement.

\textbf{Consequence.} Household parameters are justified, but a targeting policy should
weight by how much is known about each household.

\subsection{Stores are not interchangeable}

15.8\% of store-item-weeks sit more than a cent from the chain price. More
importantly, the median store ever sold only 63\% of the catalogue.

\textbf{Consequence.} Scoring an item a store never stocked as a \emph{rejected}
alternative is a specification error --- it was not there to reject.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

Household $i$, day $t$, week-of-year $w$, store $s$, item $j$, category $c=\mathrm{cat}(j)$.

\subsection{Item utility}

Everything is built on one quantity:
\begin{equation}
u_{ijt}
= \lambda_j
+ \theta_i^{\top}\alpha_j
+ \rho_j^{\top}\bar\alpha_j(S)
- \bigl(\gamma_i^{\top}\beta_j\bigr)\,\Delta\log p_{jst}
+ \eta_j^{\top}x_{ijt}
+ \mu_j^{\top}\delta_w
+ \zeta_j^{\top}\xi_s .
\label{eq:util}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
term & meaning \\
\midrule
$\lambda_j$                    & item popularity \\
$\theta_i^{\top}\alpha_j$      & household taste $\times$ item attributes \\
$\rho_j^{\top}\bar\alpha_j(S)$ & interaction with the rest of the basket \\
$-(\gamma_i^{\top}\beta_j)\Delta\log p_{jst}$ & price, with per-household sensitivity \\
$\eta_j^{\top}x_{ijt}$         & how long since this household last bought this kind of thing \\
$\mu_j^{\top}\delta_w$         & seasonality \\
$\zeta_j^{\top}\xi_s$          & store $\times$ item affinity \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The price coefficient $\gamma_i^{\top}\beta_j$ is a low-rank bilinear form, so every
household gets its own sensitivity and every item its own loading at $(N+J)K_p$
parameters instead of $NJ$. It enters with a minus sign, so a \emph{positive} value means
demand falls when price rises.

\subsection{The memory term}

Let $\tau_{ijt}$ be the days since household $i$ last bought item $j$'s
\emph{sub-commodity}. Then
\begin{equation}
x_{ijt} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbb{1}\{\text{no previous purchase}\}\\
e^{-\tau/7}\\
e^{-\tau/g_{\mathrm{sub}(j)}}\\
\log(1+\tau)/\log 100
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with the last three set to zero when there is no previous purchase. $g_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is
that sub-commodity's median repurchase gap, so ``a long time'' means the right number of
days for milk and for shampoo.

\subsection{The basket interaction}

For item $j$ in a basket $S$ of $n$ items,
\begin{equation}
\bar\alpha_j(S) \;=\; \frac{1}{n-1}\Bigl(\sum_{k\in S}\alpha_k-\alpha_j\Bigr),
\qquad \bar\alpha_j(S):=\mathbf{0} \text{ when } n=1,
\end{equation}
so the interaction contributes $\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k\neq j}\rho_j^{\top}\alpha_k$: the
average affinity between $j$ and each other item in the basket.

\paragraph{Tied versus untied.} With $\rho_j=\alpha_j$ the interaction is
$\alpha_j^{\top}\alpha_k$: symmetric, and large exactly when two items are \emph{alike}.
That forces co-purchase structure into $\alpha$, which is what makes the embedding
recover product categories (Section~\ref{sec:embed}), but it cannot express
complementarity --- two items that go together \emph{because} they are different. Untying
$\rho$ from $\alpha$ follows SHOPPER \cite{shopper}: the effect of $k$ on $j$ is
$\rho_j^{\top}\alpha_k$, which need not equal $\rho_k^{\top}\alpha_j$ and may be negative,
so complements and substitutes both become representable. Section~\ref{sec:cooc} measures
the trade.

\paragraph{The joint this implies (tied case).} Write $b_j$ for everything in
Eq.~\ref{eq:util} not involving other basket items, and define for baskets of size $n$
\begin{equation}
E(S) \;=\; \sum_{j\in S} b_j \;+\; \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\{j,k\}\subset S}\alpha_j^{\top}\alpha_k .
\end{equation}
Swapping item $j$ for item $m$ keeps $|S|=n$, so $1/(n-1)$ is identical on both sides and
\begin{equation}
E(S')-E(S)
= (b_m-b_j) + \alpha_m^{\top}\bar\alpha_j(S) - \alpha_j^{\top}\bar\alpha_j(S)
= u_m-u_j ,
\end{equation}
which is exactly what the choice softmax scores. So the item head's conditionals are those
of $P(S)\propto e^{E(S)}$ at fixed basket size, and Gibbs sampling --- resample one slot
at a time from the current draft --- converges to it. This identity is verified
numerically in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\subsection{The nest}

Category volume and item allocation factor as a Poisson--multinomial:
\begin{align}
Q_{ict} &\sim \mathrm{Poisson}\bigl(\exp(a_{ic}+\kappa_c\, IV_{ict})\bigr), \\
(q_{ijt})_{j\in c}\mid Q_{ict} &\sim \mathrm{Multinomial}\bigl(Q_{ict},\ \mathrm{softmax}_j\, u_{ijt}\bigr), \\
IV_{ict} &= \log\!\!\sum_{j\in c,\ \text{stocked at }s}\!\! e^{u_{ijt}} .
\end{align}
This factorises into independent per-item counts
\begin{equation}
q_{ijt}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\bigl(\exp(a_{ic}+(\kappa_c-1)IV_{ict}+u_{ijt})\bigr),
\end{equation}
which shows what $\kappa_c$ means: at $\kappa=1$ the inclusive value cancels and category
volume is whatever the items sum to; at $\kappa=0$ category volume is fixed and a price
cut only moves share; at $\kappa>1$ the category expands more than proportionally.
$\kappa_c=\mathrm{softplus}(\kappa^{\text{raw}}_c)$ is initialised at exactly $1.0$ so the
data must move it in either direction.

\subsection{Four heads}

\paragraph{Item.} A softmax over the true item plus 20 sampled negatives, with
unavailable items masked out:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{item}}
= -\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{r\in R}\log
\frac{e^{u_{j_r}}}{\sum_{m\in C_r}\mathbb{1}\{\text{avail}_m\}e^{u_m}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Quantity.} With its own price coefficient, so the two margins need not share
one elasticity:
\begin{equation}
z_{ijt}=q^0_j-(\gamma^{q\top}_i\beta^q_j)\Delta\log p_{jst}+\eta^{q\top}_j x_{ijt},
\qquad \text{units}-1\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(e^{z}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Incidence.} A Bernoulli per (trip, category):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{logit}_{ict}
= c^0_c + c^{u\top}_i c^c_c + c^{s\top}_c x_{ict}
+ \kappa_c\bigl(IV_{ict}-\overline{IV}_c\bigr)
+ \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{c'\in B,\,c'\neq c} W_{cc'}}_{\text{optional, Section~\ref{sec:cooc}}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Breadth.} Given the category is bought, how many \emph{distinct} items:
\begin{equation}
z^b_{ic}=b^0_c+b^u_i-b^p_c\,\overline{\Delta\log p}_{ct},
\qquad (\text{distinct items})-1\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(e^{z^b}).
\end{equation}
This head is necessary rather than decorative. Deriving a count from $P(\text{buy})$ alone
assumes $Q\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ with $1-e^{-\lambda}=p$, giving
$\mathbb{E}[Q\mid Q>0]=\lambda/p$, which is between $1.01$ and $1.12$ for realistic
incidence rates --- against a real 1.284.

\subsection{Objective}

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{item}}
+w_q\mathcal{L}_{\text{qty}}+w_c\mathcal{L}_{\text{inc}}+w_b\mathcal{L}_{\text{brd}}
+\frac{1}{B}\Bigl(\ell_2\lVert\Theta_{\text{repr}}\rVert^2+\ell^p_2\lVert\Theta_{\text{price}}\rVert^2\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The two penalties are separate on purpose: shrinking the price block at the
representation block's rate biases the elasticity toward zero, and the elasticity is the
quantity being measured.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

All numbers are on the held-out test weeks. The seed spread on item log-likelihood is
\textbf{0.0032} nats, so any gap larger than about $0.01$ is real.

\subsection{Are the equations what the code computes?}

Fifteen checks compare each printed formula against the fitted model --- exactly where
the document states an identity, by finite difference where it states a derivative.
All pass: the utility term by term; the context formula; the $E(S')-E(S)=u_m-u_j$
identity; the Poisson likelihood; $\kappa=\mathrm{softplus}$; the implied breadth
$\lambda/p$; the three elasticity derivatives; and that the sampled inclusive value is
unbiased for the sum but biased low for its logarithm by Jensen's inequality.

\subsection{Ablations}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
model & item log-lik & top-1 & cost of removing \\
\midrule
full model            & $-1.9927$ & 0.378 & --- \\
seed replicate        & $-1.9895$ & 0.379 & --- \\
no store              & $-2.1820$ & 0.355 & \textbf{0.189} \\
no interaction        & $-2.1074$ & 0.347 & \textbf{0.115} \\
no household state    & $-2.0684$ & 0.360 & \textbf{0.076} \\
prices scrambled      & $-2.0416$ & 0.362 & 0.049 \\
availability mask only& $-1.9932$ & 0.375 & 0.001 \\
no breadth            & $-1.9927$ & 0.378 & 0.000 \\
no quantity           & $-1.9925$ & 0.378 & 0.000 \\
no nest               & $-1.9877$ & 0.380 & $-0.005$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Three heads cost nothing on item ranking, and the nest is marginally better
without.} This is the design working, not failing: the item head ranks items, while the
nest, quantity and breadth heads answer questions the item head never asks. They share
only $u_{ijt}$. A head that improved item ranking \emph{because} it also modelled
incidence would mean the two were entangled. Each head must therefore be scored on its
own quantity --- the breadth head is worth $0.000$ nats here and yet fixes the only
generation criterion that was failing.

\paragraph{Stores, split honestly.} Of the $0.189$ nats the store information is worth,
\textbf{99.7\%} is the availability mask, which merely makes the choice set smaller.
Modelling stores was still correct, but it is a correctness fix, not an information gain.

\subsection{Against simple alternatives}

Every model scored through \emph{one} scorer on identical candidate sets: same positives,
same negatives, same availability mask, 51,167 held-out purchases with 20
negatives each. Baseline weights are tuned on validation.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
model & log-lik & top-1 \\
\midrule
\textbf{nested}                 & \textbf{$-2.0011$} & \textbf{0.371} \\
nested, no interaction          & $-2.1195$ & 0.342 \\
household repeat-purchase       & $-2.3428$ & 0.285 \\
popularity                      & $-2.7375$ & 0.091 \\
random                          & $-2.7378$ & 0.067 \\
household $+$ co-occurrence     & $-3.0526$ & 0.182 \\
item--item co-occurrence        & $-3.0655$ & 0.078 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The strongest baseline is not popularity but \emph{household repeat-purchase}. Grocery is
repetitive and most of what any model can predict is that people rebuy what they always
rebuy. The real margin is \textbf{$+0.342$ nats and 8.5 points of top-1}.

Two results cut against the model. Popularity is indistinguishable from random here,
because negatives are drawn popularity-weighted --- so ``we beat popularity'' is a weaker
claim than it sounds. And raw item--item co-occurrence scores \emph{below} random: the
signal exists but counting cannot exploit it against popularity-matched decoys.

\paragraph{What this number is.} It is a \emph{conditional} likelihood: the probability of
one item given the rest of the basket. Test baskets are from held-out weeks so nothing
leaks across time, but this is a fill-in-the-blank score, not ``predict the basket''. The
from-scratch test is Section~\ref{sec:gen}.

\subsection{Is the price response causal?}

We refit the entire model on a price panel scrambled before fitting, including the
store-level deviations --- leaving those real would leak genuine prices back in.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
model & price coefficient & quantity price coef. & $\kappa$ \\
\midrule
fitted            & \textbf{$+0.794$} & $+0.134$ & 0.663 \\

\subsection{Where a price cut goes}

From the softmax and the Poisson--multinomial, a 1\% cut in item $j$'s price moves demand
through three channels:
\begin{align}
\text{allocation:}\quad & \frac{\partial\log\pi_j}{\partial\log p_j}
= -\bigl(\gamma_i^{\top}\beta_j\bigr)\bigl(1-\pi_j\bigr), \\
\text{incidence:}\quad & \kappa_c\frac{\partial IV}{\partial\log p_j}
= -\kappa_c\bigl(\gamma_i^{\top}\beta_j\bigr)\pi_j, \\
\text{quantity:}\quad & \frac{\partial\log\mathbb{E}[\text{units}]}{\partial\log p_j}
= -\bigl(\gamma^{q\top}_i\beta^q_j\bigr)\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda},\quad \lambda=e^{z}.
\end{align}
The first two are exact derivatives of the same softmax, which is why they carry
$(1-\pi_j)$ and $\pi_j$ and why they combine to
$-(\gamma^{\top}\beta)[1-\pi_j(1-\kappa_c)]$. Evaluated on 12,724 held-out purchases:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
channel & elasticity & share \\
\midrule
allocation (share within category) & $-0.991$ & 83\% \\
incidence (category expands)       & $-0.058$   & 5\% \\
quantity (units per buyer)         & $-0.139$   & 12\% \\
\midrule
total (mean)                       & $-1.188$   & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Mostly it moves share. The quantity margin is 12\% --- a share a binary-purchase
model cannot reach at all, and independently corroborated by the model-free
units-per-buyer elasticity of $-0.235$ in Section~\ref{sec:eda}. Note $\kappa=0.663$
moved across three incidence samplers before settling, so it is weakly identified and no
argument here rests on its exact value.

\subsection{The co-occurrence investigation}
\label{sec:cooc}

Generated baskets carried only 43\% of the real co-occurrence lift. Three separate
causes were found, each by measurement.

\paragraph{Cause 1: the wrong level.} The item-level Gibbs sweep resamples a slot
\emph{from its own category}, because that is the move $E(S)$ is defined over. Splitting
generated pairs by whether the sweep can reach them:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
pair type & pairs & generated / real lift & Spearman vs real \\
\midrule
same category (reachable)  & 230   & 49\% & \textbf{$+0.290$} \\
cross category (not)       & 4,047 & 45\% & \textbf{$-0.094$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{95\% of item pairs are cross-category}, and which categories entered a basket was
decided by $C$ independent Bernoulli draws. So most co-purchase structure was set by
independent coin flips and no item-level work could reach it.

\paragraph{Cause 2: dilution.} $\bar\alpha$ divides by $n-1$, so a partner worth
$\alpha_j^{\top}\alpha_k=6$ contributes $6/7$ in an eight-item basket. Given a free
scalar on the interaction, the model sets it to \textbf{5.5} --- close to the average
$n-1$.

\paragraph{Cause 3: tied embeddings encode similarity, not complementarity.}
$\alpha^{\top}\alpha$ averages $4.34$ within a sub-commodity and $0.47$ across categories.
Given a \emph{tied} category-level context the model fitted its scale to $-3.0$: it used
the term for basket-size competition, because it could not use it for complementarity.

The escape at category level is arithmetic. With $C=188$, a full symmetric pairwise
matrix $W$ is 17,578 free parameters --- cheaper than blocks already in the model,
and impossible at item level ($J^2 \approx 30$M). $W$ can place two categories at $+2$
while their embeddings sit far apart.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
variant & item log-lik & inc.\ NLL & $\kappa$ & lift & same-sub & price \\
\midrule
base                       & $-1.9927$ & 0.1104 & 0.663 & 43\% & 57\% & 82\% \\
$+$ learnable scale        & $-1.9966$ & 0.1116 & 0.658 & 44\% & 80\% & 80\% \\
$+$ category context       & $-1.9970$ & 0.1075 & 0.828 & 44\% & 89\% & 84\% \\
$+$ pair matrix, weak $L_2$& $-1.9971$ & 0.0987 & 0.597 & 49\% & 121\% & 70\% \\
$+$ pair matrix, $L_2=0.3$ & $-1.9966$ & 0.1051 & 0.665 & 44\% & 86\% & 81\% \\
\textbf{both, regularised} & $-1.9970$ & 0.1012 & 0.815 & 46\% & 92\% & 87\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Co-occurrence competes with price.} Unregularised, the pair matrix is the best
model of category co-occurrence and drags the price response down to 70\%. The
mechanism is visible in $\kappa$, which falls to 0.597: price reaches incidence
\emph{only} through $\kappa\cdot IV$, so any mechanism explaining co-occurrence more
cheaply takes that job and the price signal with it. Penalising the matrix separately ---
the treatment the price block already gets --- restores both.

\paragraph{Stacking escapes the trade-off.} The tied category context pushes $\kappa$
\emph{up} while the pair matrix pushes it \emph{down}; together, regularised, the model
holds $\kappa$ at 0.815 and improves four of the five checks at a cost inside the seed
spread.

%======================================================================
\section{What still fails}
\label{sec:fail}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The pattern of co-occurrence is not learned.} Every variant gets the
\emph{amount} of clustering closer to real and none gets the per-pair rank correlation
away from zero. The model knows baskets should be coherent; it does not know that hot
dogs go with buns specifically.

\item \textbf{Promotion weeks are steeper than the model's price term.} Section~\ref{sec:eda}
measures an implied slope of $-2.83$ in the cut week against $-0.792$ on ordinary
variation. A promoted price arrives with display and feature that the price variable does
not carry, so a single coefficient must average two regimes.

\item \textbf{Basket-size distribution.} Means match to a hundredth; the distributions sit
0.30 apart in total variation. Real baskets are far more skewed.

\item \textbf{No held-out joint likelihood.} $P(S)$ needs a partition function over all
size-$n$ baskets, which is intractable to evaluate. The reported item log-likelihood is
conditional on the rest of the basket and is not a substitute.

\item \textbf{$\kappa$ is weakly identified.} It moved with the incidence sampler rather
than with the data before settling.

\item \textbf{Per-household price sensitivity is barely measurable for light shoppers.}
Split-half reliability is $+0.236$ overall and rises with data volume, so targeting
should weight by how much is known about each household.
\end{enumerate}
