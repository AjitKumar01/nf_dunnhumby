Theory · version 3

A retail environment model: baskets, prices, coupons, policy

A probabilistic model of supermarket baskets whose normaliser is exactly computable, so that a whole basket can be scored, sampled, and differentiated with respect to price. Part I states it. Part II says why each term is what it is. Parts III to VI cover estimation, what can and cannot be identified causally from this dataset, and what it takes for the result to serve as an environment for policy search. Every mathematical claim that can be checked by enumeration has been.

The objective this is written against

One model has to do four things at once. They conflict, and most of the design decisions in this document are about resolving a specific conflict between them by derivation rather than by assertion.

  1. Score a whole basket. Answer is this set of n products plausible, as a set, for this household on this day? — one number, exactly normalised.
  2. Answer price counterfactuals. Give P(basket | do(price)), not just P(basket | price), with the identifying assumptions stated and the ones that fail admitted.
  3. Support coupon targeting. Give the incremental effect on a household of receiving a coupon for a product, which is a household × product object, not a population average.
  4. Be a simulator. Be a Markov decision process whose state is closed under its own dynamics, so that a promotion policy changes the state, which changes the next basket, which changes the state again.

How to read this document

Three kinds of claim, never blended, following version 2's convention.

  • Derived — follows from stated assumptions, with the derivation given.
  • Verified — a mathematical claim checked numerically against brute force by a named script, with the residual quoted.
  • Measured — a number from the data or from a fitted model, quoted with the quantity it came from.

Modelling choices with no derivation behind them are marked in amber. Nothing here has been fitted to dunnhumby yet: this document is the theory, its verification and its feasibility analysis. Everything labelled measured is either a property of the data or a result already in this repository from the model this one replaces. Where a number would have to come from fitting this model, it says so.

Comparison with the predecessor, and the record of errors corrected during review, are in Appendix D and Appendix E. They are deliberately not in the main text: the model should stand on its own, and a reader who has never seen the earlier work should not have to.

Part I
The model, stated

1The model in one page

Everything in Parts I and II is contained in what follows. The rest of Part I defines the symbols and derives the one theorem that makes it computable; Part II says why each term is there rather than something else.

A trip is a shopping occasion by household i at store s on day t. Its outcome is a set of products S drawn from the store's assortment 𝒜s, together with a unit count qj ≥ 1 for each product in it.

Every product has a standalone value b(g)ijt — a linear index in household taste, price, promotion, coupon, seasonality, store, and the household's own purchase history (Eq. 7). g indexes a discrete trip type.

A basket's plausibility is one number. Products are not chosen one at a time and not in two stages; the whole set is scored at once by an energy, and the probability of a set is proportional to the exponential of that energy:

E(S) = ∑j ∈ S b(g)ijt  +  ∑{j,k} ⊆ S φj⊤φk  −  ∑c ρc nc(nc − 1)2  −  ρ0( n )
P( S ) = exp E(S) / Z
(the model)

Four terms, and each answers a different question about a basket.

termwhat it encodesfree to be
∑j∈S bjis this product attractive to this household today, on its ownanything a linear index can say
∑{j,k}⊆S φj⊤φkdo these two products belong together — complementarity, across categories and withinsymmetric, rank Kz, positive semi-definite

The normaliser Z sums over every subset of the assortment — about 10915 of them at the median store. Theorem 1 (§9) shows this collapses exactly to a Kz-dimensional Gaussian integral of a product of one-dimensional polynomials, computable to machine precision. That single fact is what makes the rest possible: an exact likelihood to maximise, exact sampling with no Markov chain, and exact derivatives of any purchase probability with respect to any price.

Units are a separate factor (§10), P(S, q) = P(S) ∏j∈S P(qj), which follows from a stated conditional-independence assumption rather than from an ordering of decisions.

Trips are generated too (§26), by a hazard on the household's own state, so a promotion can change when someone shops and not only what they buy.

1.1What the model does not have, deliberately

  • No composition stage. Which categories are entered, how many products come from each, and how large the basket is are all consequences of E, not separate draws that precede it. A model in which complementarity cannot change what you enter the aisle for is not modelling complementarity.
  • No sequence and no ordering. A basket is a set. The model assigns it one probability, not one per order in which it might have been assembled.
  • No nesting coefficient, no inclusive value. The category-level quantities that would need them are derivatives of log Z, available in closed form (Eq. 17).
  • No latent quantity that is reconstructed and then conditioned on. Anything unobserved is either integrated out exactly — the basket effect z, the trip type g — or defined from observables. This is Axiom O (§5.1), and it is why there is no inventory variable.

5.1The state, and the one axiom this design turns on

A trip's conditioning information splits into two kinds, and version 2 does not distinguish them. Making the distinction is what turns a conditional model into a simulator.

Axiom S — closure of the state

Every quantity the basket law conditions on must be either (a) an exogenous input supplied from outside — calendar, and the retailer's actions — or (b) a deterministic function of the household's own simulated history. Nothing may be a lookup frozen at the training window.

Axiom O — provenance of every state variable

Axiom S alone is not enough, and an earlier draft of this document treated it as though it were. A quantity may enter the model in exactly one of three ways:

  1. Observed — it is in the data; use it.
  2. Latent and integrated out — it is not in the data, so sum or integrate over it, as Eq. 12 does for z and Eq. 27 for g.
  3. Defined — it is a stated deterministic function of observed quantities, with no free dynamics of its own.

What is forbidden is a fourth thing: reconstructing an unobserved quantity from a recursion whose rate and initial condition are not identified, and then conditioning on the reconstruction as though it were observed.

This document previously did the forbidden thing, with inventory

An earlier draft carried a per-category inventory state Iic,t+1 = max(0, Iict − ric) + ∑j∈𝒞(c)qijt and put it in the product value (Eq. 7) and the trip hazard (Eq. 33). dunnhumby contains no inventory data. Purchases are the inflow; consumption is the outflow and is never observed; the stock at the start of the panel is never observed. So that recursion has an unidentified rate ric and an unidentified initial condition, and both are "identified" only through the functional form of the very model they feed — the model's fit is the only evidence about the rate, and the rate is an input to the fit.

Three further reasons it was wrong, beyond the identification circle:

  • It is a belief, not a state. If stock is latent, the Markov state is the posterior over it and the control problem is a POMDP. Substituting a point reconstruction is a certainty-equivalence step that is not generally valid.
  • It was inconsistent with the rest of the model. Two latent variables, z and g, are integrated out exactly. A third was plugged in. There is no principled reason for the asymmetry.
  • It is near-unfalsifiable. With a constant rate, the reconstruction is close to a smooth function of "how much was bought" and "how long ago" — quantities already in the recency features. Any apparent gain could be the extra flexibility rather than inventory, and nothing in the design separated the two.

Version 2 violates this. Its persistence terms ωij (within-category purchase share) and fij (log purchase count) are computed once on training data and never updated, so a simulated household can never become loyal to a product it starts buying. Its recency features are read from the real history. Under Axiom S these become recursions. Writing nτij for a discounted purchase count with decay ϱ per day and qijt for units bought:

nτij,t+1 = ϱ · nτijt + 1{ j bought on day t } ,    ωijt = nτijtmax(1, ∑j′ ∈ 𝒞(c(j)) nτij′t) ,    fijt = log(1 + nτijt)log 100
(3)

What inventory was there to do — let a large purchase suppress the next one, so that a promotion can pull demand forward rather than create it — is kept, but as a defined quantity in the sense of Axiom O rather than a reconstructed one. Write Δtic for the days since household i last bought from category c, Qic for the units it took on that occasion, and gc for the category's median repurchase gap over the training window. Define the days-of-supply proxy

All three ingredients are observed: units from purchase lines, elapsed days from trip dates, and gc from the empirical distribution of gaps — a descriptive statistic, not a fitted rate. There is no free depletion rate and no initial condition. The loading on xsup in Eqs. 7 and 33 stays free, which is a regression coefficient and not a latent dynamic.

The full state is hit = ( {nτijt}j, {Qic, Δtic}c, {Δtiσ}σ, tilast ) — discounted counts, last-occasion units and elapsed days per category, days since last purchase of each sub-commodity, and the day of the last trip. Every component is a function of the household's own observed or simulated purchases, so the process is Markov in h by construction.

What the substitution keeps, what it loses, and how to tell whether it earns its place

Keeps the mechanism that matters for policy: a promotion that moves three units instead of one raises Qic, which lowers the next trip's hazard and the category's value on it. Forward-buying and its payback are representable, which is what a markdown policy must not be allowed to ignore.

Loses any claim to a stock level, any household-specific consumption rate beyond the common gc scaling, and the accumulation of stock across several successive purchases.

Testable, and it should be tested before it is kept. Version 2's recency features already contain Δt and gσ through e−Δt/gσ(j). The only new information in Eq. 4 is Qic — how much was bought, not just when. So the question is sharp: does knowing the size of the last purchase predict the next one, given its timing? If not, Eq. 4 should be dropped and the model left with recency alone. This is test 8 in §32 and it costs one held-out comparison.

Exponential decay in Eq. 3 is likewise a choice, and ϱ is a smoothing constant of a feature transform rather than the decay rate of anything real. It does not raise the Axiom O problem, because nτ is computable exactly from observed purchases for any ϱ.

Design B — held-out prediction. Predicting whether a previously-bought category is bought on this trip, fitted on training weeks and scored on test weeks over 141,448 held-out rows at a base rate of 0.0855: log-loss 0.234803 with a flexible function of timing alone, 0.234416 adding units. A gain of 0.000387 nats, which is 1.35% of what timing itself buys over the base rate, and about a seventieth of the 0.028-nat effect §21 treats as evidence.

The verdict, and why the two designs disagree in importance. By a predictive standard Eq. 4 does not earn its place. By a policy standard it does, and the distinction is not special pleading: the coefficient is what a counterfactual uses, not the log-loss. Units barely vary exogenously in observational data, so the predictive gain is small; a promotion deliberately moves them, and the same coefficient then applies to much larger variation. A markdown moving three units instead of one buys back roughly 2% of the next gap.

So forward-buying is representable, and it is weak. The environment will not be blind to promotional pull-forward, but the payback it represents is small, and that is a finding about this dataset rather than a property of the model. Eq. 4 is kept because it costs no parameters; no claim is made that it improves prediction.

Version 2's frozen terms were measured to cost only 0.008 on the twelve-step novelty ratio (ANALYSIS.md §2), so this is not urgent for short horizons. It is structural for long ones, and a policy-learning run is a long one.

A trip's outcome is a purchase-count vector over the store's assortment. Write x ∈ {0,1}𝒜s for the purchase indicators, so S = {j : xj = 1}, and qj ∈ {1,…,12} for the units of a purchased product. Define

with Δlog p the centred log price (Appendix B of version 2, unchanged), D and M display and mailer indicators, and dijt ∈ {0,1} the coupon eligibility indicator — 1 when household i holds a live coupon covering product j on day t. d is new in version 3 and §21 onward is about it.

Why eligibility is an indicator and not a depth

An earlier draft of this document made dijt ∈ [0,1] a fractional price reduction. That is not available. dunnhumby's coupon.csv carries exactly three fields — COUPON_UPC, PRODUCT_ID, CAMPAIGN — and coupon_redempt.csv carries household_key, DAY, COUPON_UPC, CAMPAIGN. Neither records a face value. The only discount amounts in the release are COUPON_DISC on transaction lines, which exist only where a coupon was redeemed: 36,422 lines of 2,595,732 (1.40%), median $1.00, median depth 26.8% of line value.

Imputing a face value per coupon from those redeemed lines is possible but thin — 2,318 redemption events over 1,135 coupons, about two each — and selected, because redemption is a choice. Version 3 therefore treats eligibility as binary and reports the effect per coupon held. §23 records what this costs: the coupon response can discipline the sign and existence of a price effect but cannot be rescaled to a markdown of a stated depth without an assumed face value.

One linear index per product, evaluated with no basket around it. This is version 2's b, extended by the coupon channel and the trip-type shift, and with the co-purchase loading split off into its own embedding:

Three changes from version 2's Eq. 4, each with a reason.

  • g, the trip type. A discrete latent in {1,…,G} shifting the overall level by ag and tilting toward kinds of product by ϑg. It is discrete so that it can be summed over exactly — G extra terms, no approximation. §14.3 measures how much it actually carries, which is less than one would hope.
  • The coupon term. Separate from the price term, with its own household and product loadings, because §22 identifies it from different variation and it must be allowed to differ. Since d is binary, γd⊤iβdj is the shift in product value from holding a coupon — an intent-to-treat effect on the utility scale, not a price elasticity.
  • α no longer carries co-purchase. Version 2 ties the taste embedding and the interaction embedding, which §17 of that document notes is what pins α's scale, and §10.1 notes is stronger than the theory needs. Version 3 unties them: αj ∈ ℝK carries taste, and a separate φj ∈ ℝKz carries co-purchase. The scale of φ is pinned by the interaction alone, which is cleaner. Nothing requires Kz to match K, and §14.2 shows raising it does not buy dispersion, so it is set small.

There is no u versus b distinction in version 3. Version 2 needs two evaluations of one function because its item conditional is defined with the rest of the basket in the context; here the basket law is written directly as a joint, and the conditional is a consequence (§11), not a definition.

8The energy

The whole basket law is one exponential family over subsets of the assortment. There is no composition stage.

E(S) = ∑j ∈ S b(g)ijt + ∑{j,k} ⊆ S φj⊤φk − ∑c ρc nc(nc − 1)2 − ρ0( n(S) )
(8)
P( S | g, 𝒢 ) = exp E(S)Z(g, 𝒢) ,    Z(g, 𝒢) = ∑S ⊆ 𝒜s exp E(S)
(9)

Read term by term:

  • ∑j∈S bj — each product's standalone value.
  • ∑{j,k}⊆S φj⊤φk — a pairwise interaction over every pair in the basket, within or across categories. Positive dot product means the two products make each other more likely. This is the complementarity term, and unlike version 2's it acts on whether a category is entered at all, because there is no composition to condition on.
  • −ρc nc(nc−1)/2 — a uniform extra interaction among products of the same category. Since nc(nc−1)/2 counts the within-category pairs, this adds −ρc to every same-category pair. ρc > 0 is substitution — the second yoghurt is worth less than the first; ρc < 0 is stocking up. It is a free real number, so the data decides the sign.
  • −ρ0(n) — a free function of total basket size, one value per n ∈ {0,…,nmax}, with ρ0(0) = 0 fixing the scale. §14 shows this is the term that makes the model usable as a price environment, and that version 2's 1/(n−1) divisor is a hard-coded special case of it.

Collecting the pairwise terms, the interaction matrix is

Wjk = φj⊤φk − ρc(j) 1{ c(j) = c(k) }    ( j ≠ k )
(10)

— low rank plus block diagonal, symmetric by construction, and indefinite, which §13 shows is exactly what is needed and what version 2 cannot have.

9The normaliser

Eq. 9 sums over 2|𝒜s| subsets — about 10915 at the median store. It is nevertheless computable in closed form up to a low-dimensional Gaussian integral.

Theorem 1 — the normaliser

Write b̃j = b(g)ijt − ½‖φj‖² and, for z ∈ ℝKz, wj(z) = exp( b̃j + z⊤φj ). Let er denote the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree r, and define the per-category generating polynomial

Gc(u; z) = ∑r=0min(Nsc, R) exp( −ρc r(r−1)2 )   er( { wj(z) }j ∈ 𝒞s(c) )   ur
(11)

Let An(z) be the coefficients of the product, ∏c Gc(u; z) = ∑n An(z) un. Then

Z = 𝔼z ~ N(0, IKz) [ ∑n=0nmax e−ρ0(n) An(z) ]
(12)

Proof. Three steps, each elementary.

Step 1 — complete the square. With vS = ∑j∈Sφj,

∑{j,k} ⊆ S φj⊤φk = ½ ( ‖vS‖² − ∑j ∈ S ‖φj‖² )
(13)

because ‖vS‖² = ∑j,k∈Sφj⊤φk counts each unordered pair twice and each diagonal term once. Absorbing the diagonal into b̃ gives exp E(S) = ( ∏j∈S eb̃j ) · e½‖vS‖² · e−∑cρcC(nc,2) − ρ0(n).

Step 2 — Hubbard–Stratonovich. For any v ∈ ℝKz, the moment generating function of a standard Gaussian gives

𝔼z ~ N(0,I)[ ez⊤v ] = e½‖v‖²
(14)

Substituting and exchanging the (finite) sum over subsets with the expectation,

Z = 𝔼z [ ∑S ⊆ 𝒜s ( ∏j ∈ S wj(z) ) · e−∑cρcC(nc,2) − ρ0(n) ]
(15)

The step that does the work is this one. Conditionally on z, the coupling between products has vanished: the summand is a product over j ∈ S of per-product weights, times a factor depending on S only through the counts. The interaction has been traded for a Kz-dimensional integral.

Step 3 — group by counts. The set of subsets with a given count vector (rc) contributes ∏c erc(w(c)), since er is by definition the sum of all products of r distinct weights. Hence

Z = 𝔼z [ ∑(rc) e−ρ0(∑crc) ∏c e−ρcC(rc,2) erc( w(c)(z) ) ]
(16)

The inner sum is exactly the coefficient extraction in the statement: without ρ0 it factorises as ∏cGc(1;z); with it, the constraint ∑crc = n is carried by the polynomial variable u, and summing over n with weights e−ρ0(n) gives Eq. 12. ∎

9.1Three corollaries that fall out immediately

Corollary 1 — the empty basket

P(S = ∅) = 1 / Z exactly.

Proof. E(∅) = 0 by Eq. 8 with ρ0(0) = 0, so its unnormalised weight is 1. ∎ Hence conditioning a trip on buying something costs nothing but replacing log Z with log(Z − 1) — exact, where version 2 needs the inclusion–exclusion factor 1 − ∏cP(yc=0) and estimates it from a sample of categories. Verified to relative error 3.8 × 10−15.

Corollary 2 — inclusion probabilities and the gradient

Because log Z is the cumulant generating function of the sufficient statistic x in the natural parameter b,

∂ log Z∂ bj = 𝔼[ xj ] = P( j ∈ S ) ,    ∂² log Z∂ bj ∂ bk = Cov( xj, xk )
(17)

and conditionally on z the inclusion probability has a closed form. Writing f(z) = ∑ne−ρ0(n)An(z) for the integrand of Eq. 12,

P( j ∈ S | z ) = ∂ log f(z)∂ log wj = ∑r P( nc = r | z ) · wj er−1( w(c) \ wj )er( w(c) )
(18)

The second equality says: given the category's count, which product fills a slot depends only on that category's weights; the size term enters only through the count law P(nc = r | z), which it couples across categories.

Precision — the count law is not the category's own polynomial

When ρ0 ≡ 0 the categories are conditionally independent given z, f factorises as ∏cGc(1;z), and Eq. 18's derivative reduces to ∂ log Gc/∂ log wj. With ρ0 active that reduction is false: P(nc=r|z) is no longer Gc's own normalised coefficient but its leave-one-out marginal under the coupled law,

P( nc = r | z ) ∝ Gc[r] · ∑m e−ρ0(r+m) B(−c)m
(18a)

with B(−c) the polynomial product over every category except c — obtainable for all C categories at once from the prefix and suffix products the sampler already builds, at no extra order of cost. Verified against enumeration to 3.7 × 10−15 on all four configurations including the free-form ρ0, and the identity P(j∈S|z) = ∂log f/∂log wj confirmed by finite differences to 7 × 10−11.

Corollary 3 — sampling, exact given z

A draw from Eq. 9 is three exact steps:

  1. Draw z from its posterior q(z) ∝ N(z; 0, I) · ∑ne−ρ0(n)An(z), by sampling–importance–resampling from the Laplace proposal of §14.1 and §18.
  2. Given z, draw the total size n ∝ e−ρ0(n)An(z), then the per-category counts (rc) given ∑rc = n by a backward pass over the same suffix products used to form An.
  3. Given rc, draw exactly rc products from 𝒞s(c) by the classical sequential algorithm on suffix elementary symmetric polynomials, which is exact in O(Nsc rc) and needs no rejection.

No Gibbs sweeps, no burn-in, no mixing diagnostic. Steps 2 and 3 are exact. Step 1 is not: sampling–importance–resampling is exact only in the limit of infinitely many proposal draws, and its finite-budget error is governed by the same effective sample size that §14.2 shows collapsing from 0.878 to 0.001 across the bifurcation. So the draw is exact given z, and approximate in z.

Verified: empirical frequencies over 40,000 draws match the enumerated law to total variation 0.018–0.103 against sampling-noise floors of 0.017–0.103, on all nine configurations across the two scripts. Step 1 is the step that can fail and it is the one not yet checked.

10Units

Eq. 9 gives the set. Units are a separate factor, and version 3 says exactly what that costs rather than presenting the split as a chain rule.

Assumption U — units do not feed back

The joint over (S, q) has the form P(S, q) ∝ exp E(S) · ∏j∈S hj(qj) with each hj normalised over q ≥ 1.

Consequence. Summing over q gives ∑q∏j∈Shj(qj) = 1, so the marginal of S is exactly Eq. 9 and P(S,q) = P(S)∏j∈SP(qj). The factorisation is derived from Assumption U rather than assumed as an ordering of decisions.

What it rules out. That buying three of something changes which other products appear. It is testable: regress a basket's composition residual on its unit counts.

Given the assumption, the units law needs to fit one thing — the conditional distribution of q given purchase. Measured on training rows: P(q=1) = 0.7768, mean 1.3564, variance 0.8387. The raw variable is under-dispersed (0.62) while q − 1 is over-dispersed (mean 0.3564, variance 0.8387, ratio 2.35). Version 2's shifted Poisson forces Var(q) = 𝔼[q] − 1 and cannot do this; a shifted negative binomial, already fitted in this repository at r̂ = 2.03, reduces units-per-line total variation from 0.045 to 0.016. Version 3 keeps it:

11What the conditionals look like, and why they are not the definition

Version 2 defines the model by its single-position conditional and then proves by Brook's telescoping argument that at most one joint has those conditionals. Version 3 defines the joint and reads the conditionals off, which is the direction that cannot go wrong. For j ∉ S, comparing S ∪ {j} with S:

log P(S ∪ {j})P(S) = b(g)ijt + ∑k ∈ S φj⊤φk − ρc(j) nc(j)(S) − [ ρ0(n+1) − ρ0(n) ]

Every term is interpretable and each is the derivative of one modelling decision. The product's own value; the total pull of everything already in the basket; a penalty proportional to how many of the same category are already there; and the marginal cost of one more line. The normaliser cancels, so this is exact and cheap — which is what makes Gibbs sampling available as a check on Corollary 3, even though it is not needed.

Note on Hammersley–Clifford, and on what is not being invoked

Nothing in Part II uses Hammersley–Clifford or Besag's positivity condition. The joint is written down first and shown to be summable; the conditionals are consequences. Version 2 needed a uniqueness theorem because it started from conditionals, and its §10 correctly notes that Besag's positivity fails on its support and that Brook's argument is the right one. That entire discussion is unnecessary here, which is a property of the construction and not a claim of novelty.

12The response identity

This is the result that ties everything else together, and it is the reason the distributional failures in §3 are not cosmetic.

Proposition 1 — the basket-size response is a covariance, and for a uniform shift it is the variance

Let product values shift along an arbitrary direction, bj → bj + ε dj. Then

d 𝔼[n]d ε = ∑j,k dk ∂² log Z∂bj∂bk = Cov( n , d⊤x )     and in particular     d 𝔼[n]d δ = Var( n )   when d ≡ 1
(21)

Proof. Immediate from Corollary 2: log Z is the cumulant generating function, so 𝔼[n] = ∑j∂log Z/∂bj and differentiating again along d gives ∑j,kdkCov(xj,xk) = Cov(∑jxj, d⊤x). ∎

Verified for the uniform case by finite differences against enumeration on three configurations: relative error 1.1–1.5 × 10−10, at the finite-difference floor.

The distinction matters, because a uniform proportional price change does not produce a uniform shift in b: by Eq. 7, ∂bj/∂log pj = −γi⊤βj varies by product. So the object of interest is the covariance form, and the identity d𝔼[n]/dδ = Var(n) is the special case where every product's price sensitivity is equal. What carries the argument in the general case is Cauchy–Schwarz:

| Cov( n , d⊤x ) | ≤ sd( n ) · sd( d⊤x )
(22)

so the aggregate basket-size response to any price change is bounded by the standard deviation of basket size times that of the price-weighted purchase vector — and for a uniform shift the bound is Var(n) itself. Under-producing Var(n) caps the achievable response whatever the loadings look like — the uniform case is where the cap binds exactly, and it is the case a chain-wide markdown approaches.

What this means for every model in this class

Distributional fidelity and counterfactual validity are the same property here, not two desiderata to be traded off. For a uniform value shift the two are the same number; for a general one they are linked by Eq. 22, so a model that under-produces the basket-size variance cannot produce the aggregate response whatever its price loadings.

Measured with care about which variance: Proposition 1 is an identity for the variance under a single Z, i.e. conditional on the trip descriptor. The comparable target is therefore not the raw 10.62 pooled across 157,464 trips — 59% of which §3 attributes to covariates the model conditions on — but the residual 4.35. Version 2's generated size distribution is close to Poisson (median 6 against a mean of 7.47, p90 14), i.e. a conditional dispersion near 1, so its aggregate basket-size response to a uniform price change is too small by a factor of about four. This has never been measured on that model, and it should be: it is a one-line check that requires no refit, and if it fails, the reported total elasticity of −1.21 (or −0.90 with promotion controls) is missing its largest component.

13What a Gaussian latent can and cannot represent

Theorem 1 works because the attractive part of the interaction in Eq. 10 is ΦΦ⊤, positive semi-definite of rank Kz. That is not an incidental convenience; it is exactly the class of interactions reachable this way, and it is worth being precise about the boundary.

Proposition 2 — the reach of a real Gaussian latent

A model of the form P(S) ∝ ∫ ∏j∈Swj(z) dμ(z) with wj(z) = exp(b̃j + z⊤φj) and μ Gaussian has pairwise interaction exactly W = ΦΦ⊤ ⪰ 0. Consequently:

  1. every product attracts itself, φj⊤φj = ‖φj‖² ≥ 0;
  2. products aligned in φ attract, since the form is largest when φj and φk point the same way;
  3. total repulsion is bounded: ∑{j,k}⊆Sφj⊤φk = ½(‖vS‖² − ∑j∈S‖φj‖²) ≥ −½∑j∈S‖φj‖².

No choice of Φ escapes these; they are properties of the Gram form. Attempting to represent a negative-definite component by splitting W = Φ+Φ+⊤ − Φ−Φ−⊤ requires 𝔼[ei z⊤v] = e−½‖v‖², an oscillatory integrand whose Monte Carlo estimate suffers the sign problem — the estimator's variance grows exponentially in ‖v‖². That route is closed.

A correction to version 2's version of this argument

Version 2 states consequence (2) as "similarity and co-purchase are forced to share a sign … two brands of the same yoghurt sit close in α, so the model scores them as attracting." That is right for version 2, because it ties the interaction embedding to the taste embedding, so proximity in the space that predicts who buys what is the same proximity that governs co-purchase.

Untying removes that particular bind. In version 3 φ is free of α, so nothing forces two yoghurt brands close in φ — the model may place them apart and score them as weak complements or near-independent. What survives untying, and what no Gaussian latent escapes, is consequences (1) and (3): self-attraction is non-negative and total repulsion is bounded below. Those two are what force the enumeration, and this document should not have inherited (2) as though it were unconditional.

Version 2's §10.2 then argues, correctly, that within-category substitution does not need to be learned because its support already forbids buying two of the same product and its composition already fixes kc. That argument works only because the composition is conditioned on — and conditioning on the composition is precisely the defect of §3. Version 3 cannot borrow it, so it must represent substitution explicitly.

This is why the within-category term is enumerated rather than embedded. The term −ρcC(nc,2) adds a block-constant component −ρc(11⊤ − I) to W, whose eigenvalues are −ρc(m−1) once and +ρc with multiplicity m−1 — itself indefinite, and enough to make W indefinite. It is not reachable by any Gaussian latent, and it does not need to be, because grouping by nc in Step 3 of Theorem 1 sums over it exactly at cost O(Nsc·R). The two mechanisms are complementary by construction: the latent carries the attractive, low-rank, cross-category structure; the enumeration carries the repulsive, structured, within-category structure.

Where substitution is, and how much of it there is

Measured on training data, the within-category count given entry has mean 1.2844 and variance 0.5592, and its law is P(k=1) = 0.811, P(2) = 0.134, P(3) = 0.034, P(4) = 0.012. Removing (household, category) means leaves a residual dispersion of 0.251. Truncating at R = 8 discards 8.1 × 10−4 of the mass and at R = 12 discards 8 × 10−5.

The sign of ρc is not predicted here and should not be. Yoghurt brands substitute; canned soup on a stock-up trip does not. The point of the enumeration is that ρc is a free real parameter identified by an observable count distribution, where version 2's κc — its only cross-level coefficient — is identified only through a noisy estimated inclusive value and was measured not to respond as its own identification story predicts.

14Stability, and why the size term is forced

The Gaussian integral in Theorem 1 has to be evaluated. Its behaviour is governed by one matrix, and that matrix turns out to control the economics as well as the numerics.

14.1The mode equation

Write the log integrand of Eq. 12 as F(z) = −½‖z‖² + log[ ∑ne−ρ0(n)An(z) ]. By Corollary 2 applied conditionally on z,

∇F(z) = −z + ∑j πj(z) φj ,    ∇²F(z) = −I + Λ(z) ,    Λ(z) := Cov( ∑j φj xj | z ) ⪰ 0
(23)

where πj(z) = P(j ∈ S | z) from Eq. 18. Setting the gradient to zero gives a fixed point which is the self-consistent-field equation of mean-field theory:

z⋆ = ∑j πj(z⋆) φj = 𝔼[ ∑j ∈ S φj | z⋆ ]
(24)

Proposition 3 — stability

If supz λmax(Λ(z)) < 1 then F is strictly concave, Eq. 24 has a unique solution, the iteration z ← ∑jπj(z)φj is a global contraction with modulus sup λmax, and the Laplace proposal at z⋆ with covariance (I − Λ(z⋆))−1 is well defined.

Proof. ∇²F = −I + Λ ≺ 0 everywhere under the hypothesis, giving strict concavity and hence at most one stationary point; existence follows because F → −∞ as ‖z‖ → ∞. The map's Jacobian is Λ(z), so its operator norm is λmax(Λ). ∎

The condition is global. Evaluating λmax at one mode is necessary, not sufficient — the sweep below shows a case bifurcating into two modes each of which is locally stable with λmax < 1. The operational diagnostic is therefore multi-start plus the importance-sampling effective sample size, not the eigenvalue alone.

14.2The dispersion–stability identity

Now connect the numerics to Proposition 1. Take the Laplace expansion of Var(n), decomposing over the latent:

Var(n) = 𝔼z[ Var(n | z) ] + Varz( 𝔼[n | z] ) ≈ Λ0 + u⊤( I − Λ )−1 u
(25)

where, all evaluated at the mode z⋆ and writing vS = ∑j∈Sφj,

Λ0 := Var( n | z⋆ ) ,    u := Cov( n , vS | z⋆ ) ,    Λ := Cov( vS | z⋆ )
(25a)

with (I−Λ)−1 the posterior covariance of z at the mode, and u the derivative of 𝔼[n|z] in z.

Proposition 3b — the dispersion–stability bound

At Laplace order, for any loading matrix Φ and any Kz,

Var(n) ≤ Λ01 − λmax(Λ)    ⟺    λmax(Λ) ≥ 1 − Var(n | z⋆)Var(n)
(26)

Proof. With e = u/‖u‖, u⊤(I−Λ)−1u = ‖u‖²e⊤(I−Λ)−1e ≤ ‖u‖²/(1−λmax). By Cauchy–Schwarz on the conditional covariance, ‖u‖² = Cov(n, e⊤vS | z⋆)² ≤ Var(n|z⋆) · Var(e⊤vS|z⋆) = Λ0 e⊤Λe ≤ Λ0λmax. Multiplying gives u⊤(I−Λ)−1u ≤ Λ0λmax/(1−λmax); adding Λ0 gives the bound. ∎

Verified by verify_dispersion_bound.py on nine instances at Kz = 1, 2, 3 crossed with ρ0 absent, convex, and free-form: zero violations, both of the algebraic bound and of the exact enumerated marginal variance.

An earlier draft of this document got this wrong, and the error is worth keeping on the page

It wrote Λ0 = ∑jπj(1−πj) and, via Λ0 ≈ 𝔼[n], concluded unconditionally that dispersion D forces λmax ≥ 1 − 1/D.

∑π(1−π) is Var(n|z) only when the purchase indicators are conditionally independent given z — and ρc and ρ0 are exactly what destroys that. Counterexample: set φ = 0, so λmax = 0 exactly, and choose ρ0 so that P(n) puts half its mass at 0 and half at 12 over twelve products. Then Var(n) = 36 while ∑π(1−π) = 3: the old bound is violated twelve-fold. Worse, it contradicted the design it was written to justify — §14.4 says ρ0 frees the model to have large Var(n) at small λ, which the wrong bound forbids.

The verification did not catch it because every instance it tested ran at ρ0 ≡ 0, and because it compared the Laplace expression against a bound derived from the same wrong quantity — an algebraic identity rather than the claim. Both are now fixed and the counterexample is a permanent test.

14.2aWhat survives, and it is what the design needs

Specialise Eq. 26 to the sub-model with ρc = ρ0 = 0. There the indicators are conditionally independent given z, so Λ0 = ∑π(1−π) ≈ 𝔼[n] — accurate to a relative ∑π²/∑π, about 0.3% over an assortment of 3,041 products with a mean basket of 7.8 — and the bound becomes

14.4The size term, and what it replaces

The size direction must therefore be carried by something exactly summable rather than by the latent or a mixture. That is precisely the free function ρ0(n) in Eq. 8: it makes P(n) a free discrete distribution on {0,…,nmax}, at nmax parameters and no approximation, computed by the same generating-polynomial device as everything else. With it, λ is free to be small — the latent carries only co-purchase structure — while Var(n), and hence by Proposition 1 the aggregate price response, is fitted from data.

This is what version 2's 1/(n−1) divisor was doing

The divisor multiplies the pairwise term by 1/(n−1), which damps exactly the feedback that Proposition 3 says drives the system critical. It is an unexplained, hard-coded, one-parameter-free special case of a size potential, and version 2 records that removing it made things "substantially worse" without explaining why. Eq. 8 replaces it with a free ρ0(n) that subsumes it: any n-dependence the divisor induces can be absorbed, because ρ0 is unrestricted. The answer to "why 1/(n−1)" is that it should not be a constant chosen in advance.

Truncation: nmax = 60 covers 99.82% of training baskets and nmax = 80 covers 99.98%. Baskets above the cap are excluded from the support, which is a stated restriction of the model rather than an approximation to it.

17The likelihood

Because Z is computable, there is an actual likelihood to maximise — not a weighted composite. For a trip v with observed basket Sv and units qv:

ℓv(Θ) = log [ ∑g=1G πg(hit) exp E(g)(Sv)Z(g) − 1 ] + ∑j ∈ Sv log P( qj | j ∈ Sv )
(27)

with Z(g) − 1 from Corollary 1 implementing the conditioning on a non-empty basket, and πg a softmax over the trip-type index. The objective is −∑vℓv plus penalties. Every parameter is fitted against one objective; there are no relative weights to choose, and version 2's §11 finding that its weighting sits 24.5× away from the likelihood has no analogue here.

18Monte Carlo maximum likelihood, and its bias

Z is an expectation, estimated by importance sampling from a Laplace proposal at the mode of Eq. 24 — the machinery already built and validated on the joint-implementation branch, where it achieved effective sample size 0.70 at K = 64 and agreed with brute force to 0.002–0.007 nats. Here Kz is 8–16 rather than 64, so it should do better; that is a projection, not a measurement.

Ẑ = 1D ∑d=1D N(zd; 0, I)   f(zd)q(zd) ,    zd ~ q = N( z⋆, (I − Λ(z⋆))−1 )
(28)

where f(z) = ∑ne−ρ0(n)An(z). This is Geyer's Monte Carlo maximum likelihood, and its properties are known rather than assumed.

The bias goes the wrong way, and the honest statement of it

Ẑ is unbiased for Z, so by Jensen 𝔼[log Ẑ] ≤ log Z, so the estimated log-likelihood E(S) − log Ẑ is an upper bound in expectation on the true log-likelihood. Maximising an upper bound is the unsafe direction: the optimiser can in principle gain by making the bound loose rather than the model good.

Three things bound the damage, and all three are measurable rather than argued. First, the bias is ≈ −Var(Ẑ)/(2Z²) = −(1/D)(1/ESS − 1)/2 to leading order, so at ESS = 0.7 and D = 32 it is about 0.007 nats — a quarter of the 0.028-nat held-out improvement §21 treats as evidence, so not negligible, but it must be checked at the fitted parameters, not assumed. Second, held-out likelihood is reported with a large independent D and a fresh random seed, so model selection is not made on the same draws that were optimised. Third, ESS is logged every iteration, and §14 shows it is the diagnostic that detects the failure mode that would make the bias large.

What is not claimed: that the estimator is unbiased, or that maximising Eq. 28 is equivalent to maximum likelihood at finite D. It is not.

18.1Gradients are exact given the draws

Differentiating Eq. 28 with the proposal treated as a constant — legitimate, because importance sampling is unbiased for any fixed proposal — gives

∂ log Ẑ∂ Θ = ∑d ŵd ∂ log f(zd)∂ Θ ,    ŵd = softmax of the log importance weights
(29)

and since ∂ log f(z)/∂bj = πj(z) by Eq. 18, this is exactly the self-normalised importance-sampling estimate of the model expectation 𝔼[xj]. The gradient of the log-likelihood is therefore the familiar exponential-family form observed minus expected, xjobs − 𝔼[xj], estimated with a bias of order 1/D. No differentiation through the mode-finder is required.

20What consistency does and does not hold

Version 3 removes one of version 2's two obstructions and leaves the other exactly where it was. Saying so plainly matters more than the improvement.

  • Removed: the reweighting obstruction. Eq. 27 is a single likelihood. There is no weighted composite whose maximiser is an M-estimator for nothing in particular.
  • Not removed: incidental parameters. θi, γi, γdi, γqi and the household consumption scale si are per household — 58 parameters each at K = 32, over 2,066 households with a bounded number of trips each. This is the Neyman–Scott regime, in which the common parameters are generally inconsistent too. Version 3 does not solve it. The available treatments are a hierarchical prior with the hyperparameters estimated (partial pooling, which shrinks but does not eliminate the bias), or an explicit integral over the household effects. Neither is free and neither is implemented.
  • Added: Monte Carlo bias, §18, of order 1/D and in the unsafe direction.

What can honestly be claimed: Eq. 27 is a penalised M-estimator whose population objective is maximised at the truth as D → ∞ when the household parameters are treated as fixed effects and the number of trips per household grows. Neither condition holds here. Any interval on any parameter must come from a household block bootstrap over refits, which has never been run in this project, and not from seed spread, which version 2 §18 measured to understate uncertainty by a factor of forty.

Part IV
The causal layer

21What a counterfactual means here

Everything so far is a conditional law P(S | 𝒢). Goal 2 asks for P(S | do(price)). These coincide only under an assumption, and stating that assumption precisely is the whole content of this part.

The structural model. For a product–store–week cell, the retailer chooses a price and a promotion; households then shop. Write νcw for an unobserved demand shock at the category–week level (a holiday, a weather event, a competitor promotion) and νjsw for a product–store–week shock:

( pjsw, Djsw, Mjsw ) = F( observed history, νcw, νjsw, εretailer )   →   Sv ~ P( · | 𝒢v, ν )
(30)

The problem is the arrow from ν to both. A retailer promotes what it expects to sell; the same expectation raises demand directly. Conditioning on price therefore mixes the price response with the shock.

Assumption C — conditional ignorability of price

P(S | do(p), 𝒢) = P(S | p, 𝒢) holds if p ⫫ ν | 𝒢 — the residual price variation, after everything in Eq. 6, is unrelated to unobserved demand.

Assumption C is false in this data, and the evidence is already in the repository rather than being a suspicion.

The conclusion, stated without hedging

The shelf-price elasticity is not identified from this dataset by any modelling choice. No energy function, no normaliser, and no estimator repairs a design in which the treatment is chosen by the same agent that observes the shock and in which three quarters of the treatment is imputed. Version 2's headline elasticity should be read as a fitted association with at least two named, measured confounds, and version 3 does not change that. What version 3 changes is that it stops routing policy questions through that number.

Assumption D — the coupon exclusion

Conditional on household × week and product × week effects, coupon eligibility dijt is independent of the household's unobserved current shock to that product.

Household × week absorbs anything about that household that week — a holiday, a pay cheque, a campaign-wide attention effect. Product × week absorbs the national promotion, the seasonal peak, and the category shock νcw. What remains identifying is the interaction: among households shopping the same week, some hold a coupon for this product and some do not, and among products in the same week, a household holds coupons for some and not others.

What Assumption D still requires, and where it could fail: campaign targeting is not random — across the full release TypeA reaches 1,513 households, TypeB 1,023, TypeC 397 — and is presumably based on past behaviour. Past behaviour is observed and is in the state hit, so selection on it is handled. Selection on an unobserved household–product affinity that the retailer knows and we do not would break the assumption. That is weaker than what shelf prices require, and it is partially testable: a placebo on pre-window periods should show no effect.

The estimand is intent-to-treat, and that is the right one

We observe eligibility, not delivery, awareness, or possession. So what is identified is the effect of being sent a coupon, not of using one. With 2,318 redemptions against 142,436 eligible purchase rows, the gap between the two is enormous and any model of the second requires assumptions about awareness that this data cannot support.

For goal 3 this is fortunate rather than limiting: a targeting policy chooses whom to send coupons to. Intent-to-treat is exactly the causal quantity a targeting policy needs, and modelling redemption would answer a question nobody gets to act on. The household-specific loading γdi in Eq. 7 absorbs heterogeneous responsiveness — including the 73% of campaign households that never redeem anything — as a random coefficient rather than as a separate awareness stage.

The simulator uses tier-2 coefficients for the price channel, not tier 3. Concretely, a simulated markdown of δ log points is applied through (δ/δ̄)·γd⊤iβdj, the coupon-identified response rescaled by an assumed coupon depth δ̄, rather than through γ⊤β. This is the substantive break with version 2, which uses one price coefficient for both prediction and policy. The price of the break is the nuisance constant δ̄, which the data does not supply and which must be carried through every result as a range, exactly as §24 carries confounding and §27 carries the margin rate. A coupon-targeting policy, which chooses whether to send a coupon rather than how deep to cut, does not need δ̄ at all.

What this substitution assumes, and the fact that limits it

It assumes a coupon and a shelf markdown of the same depth move demand by the same amount. They plausibly do not: a coupon is targeted, salient, and requires an action.

And the comparison cannot be made on a common scale, because the coupon's face value is not in the data (§6). γd⊤βd is the utility shift from holding a coupon; γ⊤β is a response per log point of price. Equating them requires a depth δ̄ such that a held coupon is worth δ̄ log points, and δ̄ can only be imputed from the 36,422 redeemed lines (median depth 26.8%), which is a selected sample of a thin one.

What survives, and it is still worth having:

  • Sign and significance transfer without any face value. If the coupon-identified response is not negative on the utility scale, the shelf-price coefficient has no interventional support at all, whatever its magnitude.
  • Relative comparisons across households and products transfer. The targeting problem of §29 needs Upliftij ranked across pairs, and a common unknown scale factor δ̄ does not change a ranking.
  • An equality test is possible under an assumed δ̄, reported across the plausible range from the redemption data rather than at a point.

This test has not been run. It remains the highest-value experiment the version-3 branch can perform, and it now has a stated limitation rather than a promised equivalence.

24Sensitivity rather than false precision

Where a policy value depends on a tier-3 parameter it should be reported as a band, not a number. Parameterise the confounding by the fraction κconf ∈ [0,1] of the fitted price association attributable to unobserved demand, and report the policy value over the range. Two anchors are already measured and give the band real endpoints rather than arbitrary ones: controlling for promotion removed 31.6% of the coefficient, and the imputation contrast accounts for about 27% of the association. A band spanning κconf ∈ [0, 0.6] is therefore supported by measurement at both ends, and a policy whose ranking is stable across it can be recommended; one whose ranking flips inside it cannot.

Part V
The environment

26Trip timing

Version 2 conditions on the day and never generates it, so inter-trip gaps are exogenous and the retailer's central question — if I promote this week, does the household come in sooner? — is unanswerable. ANALYSIS.md §2 names this the largest gap. It is a discrete-time hazard on the household's own state:

P( trip on day t | hit ) = 1 − exp( −eηtripit ) ,    ηtripit = τ0i + τ1⊤xgapit + τ2⊤dow(t) − τ3⊤xsupit + τ4 Ωist
(33)

where xgap is a spline in days since the last trip, xsupit the days-of-supply vector of Eq. 4 — a low reading brings the trip forward, which is the mechanism that makes a stock-up trip followed by a long gap — and

Ωist = log ∑c exp( promotional attractiveness of category c at store s )
(34)

an exposure summary through which a promotion policy can move trip timing. Store choice given a trip is a multinomial over the stores the household has used, conditioned on the same state. Measured targets: gaps have mean 7.35 days, median 4, variance 159 (dispersion 21.6), and 18.3% of consecutive trips are on consecutive days — strongly overdispersed with a large point mass at one day, so a single-parameter geometric hazard will not fit and the spline is not decoration.

Imposed, and untested

Eq. 33 is a modelling choice with no derivation, the complementary log-log link is chosen for consistency with a latent-count reading, and the days-of-supply term assumes the category's median repurchase gap is the right scale for every household. None of this has been fitted or validated. It is included because an environment without it cannot represent the action the project exists to study, not because there is evidence for this particular form.

28How accurate does the environment have to be?

This is answerable rather than a matter of taste, and the answer is uncomfortable.

Proposition 4 — error propagation

Let M and M̂ be two decision processes with identical rewards in [0, Rmax] per step and transition kernels differing by at most ε in total variation at every reachable state–action pair. Then for any policy and horizon H,

| VπM − VπM̂ | ≤ H² Rmax ε
(35)

Proof. Coupling the two trajectory distributions step by step gives TV( trajM, trajM̂ ) ≤ H ε. The total return is bounded in [0, H Rmax], and for any f, |𝔼1f − 𝔼2f| ≤ (sup f − inf f)·TV. Multiply. ∎

Apply it. Version 2's per-trip basket-size total variation alone is 0.282, which is a lower bound on the total variation of the whole basket. At H = 12 trips and Rmax ≈ $180 (the 99.9th percentile of trip revenue), the bound gives 144 × 180 × 0.282 ≈ $7,300 against a true twelve-trip value of roughly 12 × $19.59 ≈ $235. The bound is vacuous by a factor of thirty.

What follows, and what does not

Does follow: no worst-case guarantee is available for twelve-step model-based policy evaluation at any accuracy this model class will plausibly reach. Inverting Eq. 35 for a 5% error on a twelve-step value needs ε ≤ 4.5 × 10−4, which is far below sampling noise on any held-out set this size. Reporting a simulated policy value as an estimate of the real one is not defensible, and this project should stop planning to.

Does not follow: that the environment is useless. Eq. 35 is a worst-case bound over all policies and all reward functions; it says nothing about the ranking of two similar policies, for which errors substantially cancel. The defensible workflow is: use the environment for policy search and for stress-testing, then evaluate the resulting policy on logged data by off-policy methods with their own finite-sample bounds. That division of labour is the honest one, and it is what the accuracy analysis supports rather than what one would prefer.

It also gives a concrete target that is worth hitting even so: per-trip basket total variation is the quantity that enters Eq. 35, version 2 sits at ≥ 0.28 on its coarsest marginal, and §3 attributes almost all of that to the composition stage that version 3 removes. Driving it to 0.05 would be a 5.6-fold improvement in the only bound available — which still leaves 144 × 180 × 0.05 = $1,296 against a true value near $235, so it narrows the gap without closing it.

29Coupon targeting as an optimisation

With Eq. 32 the targeting problem is well posed. Choose a binary assignment Aij ∈ {0,1} of coupons to household–product pairs:

maximise ∑i,j Aij · Upliftij    subject to   ∑j Aij ≤ Bi , ∑i,j Aij cij ≤ B , Aij = 0 for j ∉ eligible
(36)

a bipartite assignment problem with a knapsack constraint, whose linear relaxation is integral when only the per-household cardinality constraint binds and which is otherwise solved to a small gap by standard methods. The modelling work is entirely in Upliftij: it must be an interventional quantity (tier 2, §23), it must account for cannibalisation within the category and pull-through across categories (Eq. 32), and it must be a household × product object rather than a population average, which is what the household loadings γdi supply.

Note what Eq. 36 optimises: incremental revenue net of coupon cost. The uplift can be negative — a coupon on a product the household would have bought at full price is pure discount — and the model represents that correctly because Uplift subtracts the no-coupon counterfactual. That is the entire value of a causal model here, and it is why tier 3 must not be used for it.

Part VI
Assumptions, risks, and what would falsify this

30Every assumption, grouped by what fails without it

Needed for the basket law to exist and be computable

  • The interaction is symmetric. Automatic here: W in Eq. 10 is symmetric by construction. Unlike version 2 this is not a restriction imposed to repair an inherited asymmetry; it is what writing a joint means.
  • The attractive part is positive semi-definite. Required by Theorem 1's Step 2. Proposition 2 states exactly what this costs and §13 states why the repulsive part is handled by enumeration instead.
  • Truncations. nc ≤ R and n ≤ nmax restrict the support. At R = 8 this excludes 0.081% of observed category counts; at nmax = 60, 0.18% of observed baskets. These are restrictions of the model, not approximations of it, and excluded baskets must be dropped from the training set rather than clipped.

Needed for the estimates to mean anything

  • Household parameters are ancillary, or pooled, or integrated. None is supplied (§20). Unchanged from version 2 and not improved by anything here.
  • Monte Carlo bias is small at the fitted parameters (§18). Measurable, unmeasured.
  • The assortment 𝒞s(c) is what the shopper could have bought. It is defined as "sold at least once in training", which is selection on the outcome. Unchanged from version 2, and now more consequential: version 3's normaliser sums over the whole assortment, so a product wrongly excluded no longer merely shrinks a within-category denominator, it changes Z. Version 2's measured sensitivity — the allocation elasticity running from −0.57 to 0.00 as the inclusion threshold tightens — should be re-run under version 3 before any elasticity is quoted.
  • Assumption U, units do not feed back (§10). Testable, untested.

Needed for the causal claims

  • Assumption C (§21), conditional ignorability of shelf price. Believed false, with three independent pieces of measured evidence. Tier-3 parameters are therefore not used for policy.
  • Assumption D (§22), the coupon exclusion. Weaker, partially testable by a pre-window placebo, and the basis of everything interventional in this document.
  • Coupon and shelf-price responses are exchangeable, if a coupon-identified coefficient is used to simulate a markdown (§23). Testable and untested; the highest-value experiment available.

Needed for the environment

  • Axiom S, state closure, and Axiom O, provenance (§5.1). Satisfied by construction after the inventory state was removed; the cost is the imposed form of Eqs. 3 and 4, which is unvalidated.
  • The four substitutions of §5.2 — imputed prices, outcome-defined assortment, assumed coupon depth, assumed margin. Each is information the data does not contain.
  • The trip hazard, Eq. 33. Imposed entirely.
  • An assumed margin rate for any margin objective (§27).

31Risks, honestly ranked

  1. The stability risk is the largest, and it is unresolved. §14 shows the model must operate somewhere the estimator is known to degrade in enumerable toy systems, and the size potential ρ0 is the proposed escape. What is verified is that ρ0 makes the size law free and the normaliser exact. What is not established is that a fit on real data will actually place λ low once ρ0 is free to absorb the size variance — the optimiser could still route dispersion through φ. The mitigation is an explicit penalty on λmax(Λ) during fitting plus per-iteration ESS logging, and the first experiment on the branch should be to check whether the penalty binds.
  2. Assumption D could fail and there is no clean test that it does not. A pre-window placebo is necessary but not sufficient. If it fails, version 3 has no interventional tier at all and the environment degrades to a forecaster.
  3. The size potential could overfit. ρ0 has nmax ≈ 60 free values and can reproduce the observed size distribution almost by construction. That is the point, but it means basket-size agreement stops being evidence for the model — it becomes a fitted target. Evidence must come from the quantities ρ0 does not control: co-occurrence structure, cross-price signs, and held-out set likelihood.
  4. Forward-buying may not be representable at all. §5.1 removed the inventory state because dunnhumby has no inventory data, and replaced it with a proxy whose only new content is the size of the last purchase. If test 8 fails, the environment cannot represent promotional pull-forward, and every markdown policy it evaluates will over-value promotions — seeing the lift and never the payback. That is a directional bias, not noise, and it is the most policy-relevant gap that remains.
  5. The coupon face value does not exist in the data (§6), so any result expressed per log point of price carries an assumed δ̄. Rankings and signs survive; magnitudes do not. This was discovered while checking the document and it changed §23 from an equivalence test into a bounded one.
  6. Monte Carlo maximum likelihood maximises an upper bound (§18).
  7. Neyman–Scott (§20), inherited and unaddressed.

But those instances use random loadings, where λmax never exceeded 0.556. §14.2's bifurcation needed the aligned worst case φj = a for every product, and that case has not been re-run with ρ0 on. So it is not yet shown that the size term prevents the bifurcation — only that the estimator is comfortable where λ is moderate.
  • SIR is now exercised and passes, in an easy regime. verify_estimator.py runs the real thing — Laplace proposal, importance weights, resample one — and matches the enumerated law to the sampling-noise floor in all twelve configurations, at 4, 32 and 256 proposal draws alike. That four proposals suffice is a consequence of ESS ≈ 1 in these instances, not evidence that SIR is robust: the low-ESS regime where it would fail is precisely the one these instances never reach.

An earlier version of this appendix claimed only the first of these. Two of the three gaps are now closed and the third — the aligned worst case with ρ0 on — is named rather than left implicit.

DRelation to prior work, and to version 2

This appendix is where the comparative material lives. Nothing in Parts I to VI depends on it: the model is stated and justified on its own terms, and a reader who has never seen the predecessor can skip this. It is here because the predecessor exists in this repository, its numbers are cited throughout as measurements, and the reasons it was replaced are evidence rather than opinion.

D.1What version 2 is

Version 2 factorises a basket ℬ into a composition 𝒦 (which categories, how many distinct products from each), a content set S, and units q:

P( ℬ | 𝒢, n ≥ 1 ) = ∏c P(yc) · ∏c : yc=1 P(kc | yc=1) · P(S | 𝒦) · ∏j ∈ S P(qj | S)1 − ∏c P(yc = 0)
(v2)

Entry yc is an independent complementary log-log draw per category; breadth kc is a truncated shifted Poisson; the content set is a Gibbs measure on the baskets matching 𝒦,

E(S) = ∑j ∈ S bijt + 1n − 1 ∑{j,k} ⊂ S αj⊤αk ,    P(S | 𝒦) ∝ exp E(S)
(v2′)

and units are shifted Poisson. It is fitted by a weighted sum of four objectives, of which the content term is Besag's pseudo-likelihood.

D.2Where it comes from, and what was dropped in transit

The interaction in Eq. v2′ is shopper's. In Ruiz, Athey and Blei (2020), the deterministic utility of item c at position i of an ordered basket is

Ψ(c, yt,i−1) = ψtc + ρc⊤ ( 1i − 1 ∑j=1i−1 αytj )
(S1)

and the basket is generated sequentially, one item at a time, each drawn from a softmax over the items not yet taken, ending when a special checkout item is chosen. Two properties of that design are load-bearing and neither survived the transfer.

  • shopper does not need symmetry. Its interaction ρc⊤αyj is asymmetric — the effect of j on c differs from the effect of c on j. That is legitimate because shopper is an autoregressive model over a sequence, and a sequence model needs no potential function. Version 2 is a random field over a set, so a joint exists only if the interaction is symmetric; it therefore ties ρ = α. Tying is not a simplification, it is a repair, and it costs the freedom to have similar products repel (§13).
  • shopper generates the basket size. The checkout item ends the trip, so how many items a basket has is produced by the same utilities as which items it has. Version 2 replaces checkout with a separate composition stage — independent per-category coin flips and a Poisson breadth count — that the interaction never touches.

The 1/(i−1) divisor is also shopper's, where it is a per-position average over the items so far. Version 2 carries it across as 1/(n−1) with n the final basket size, which it must, because the swap identity requires the divisor to be constant across the baskets being compared. Version 2 §10.2 records that removing it was "substantially worse" and that "why it should be is not established." §14 below establishes what it was doing.

The per-category structure — at most a few products per category, additive across categories — is Donnelly, Ruiz, Blei and Athey (2023). There it is a modelling assumption about categories being separate choice problems. Version 2 keeps the structure and adds an interaction across categories, which is the right ambition, but places that interaction strictly inside a composition drawn without it.

D.3The defect, stated precisely

The incoherence

Eq. v2 is a valid probability distribution — any P(𝒦)·P(S|𝒦) is. The defect is not that it fails to normalise. It is that the joint it defines has a marginal over compositions,

Pv2(𝒦) = ∏c P(yc) · ∏c : yc=1 P(kc|yc=1)
(1)

which is unrelated to the composition marginal that the energy E itself implies,

PE(𝒦) ∝ ∑S ∈ 𝒮(𝒦) exp E(S) .
(2)

The parameters α are estimated as though they described complementarity, but they are estimated only from Eq. v2′, which is conditional on 𝒦. An interaction that cannot change what composition you buy is not complementarity. Bread and butter are in different categories; whether a household buys butter at all is decided by an independent coin flip that never sees the bread.

This is not a stylistic objection. It predicts exactly where the fitted model fails, and the prediction is already confirmed by results in this repository. Comparing generated with real baskets on held-out trips (out/generator_eval_ps_nested.json, total variation distance, four sweeps):

The two composition quantities are wrong by a factor of four to six more, and wrong in a specific way: the generated median is higher than the real median while the generated 90th percentile is much lower. That is not a bias, it is a missing spread. The generated distribution is close to Poisson; the real one is not.

Measured: the size of the missing spread

On 157,464 training trips, basket size has mean 7.803 and variance 82.842 — a dispersion index of 10.62. Categories per basket: mean 6.075, variance 40.833, dispersion 6.72. Conditional on the trip descriptor, version 2 draws C independent Bernoulli entries, and a sum of independent indicators is Poisson-binomial, whose variance ∑π(1−π) never exceeds its mean ∑π. Conditional dispersion cannot exceed 1 by construction. All observed spread must therefore be produced by variation in the linear predictor across trips, and the generated quantiles show that it is not.

Removing household means leaves categories-per-basket dispersion at 5.30, so three quarters of the excess over 1 is within household and cannot be explained by household heterogeneity. Conditioning further on day-of-week and days-since-last-trip — covariates the model has — explains 59% of the basket-size variance in total, leaving a residual dispersion of 4.35. That residual is what a latent mechanism must supply, and §14 shows it is the binding constraint on the whole design.
