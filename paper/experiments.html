Experiments

What the fitted model does, and does not, deliver

Everything here is fitted to the specification in model_spec.html: the item conditional is summed exactly over the category, so no number depends on a negative-sampling scheme. Five results are negative and are reported as such — including the two that decide whether the model is deployable.

1Protocol, and what is comparable

All numbers are on held-out test weeks. Eight models were fitted, differing only in which term is removed, all for 6,000 iterations with identical data and schedule. The item score is the exact conditional of Eq. 18 of the specification: a softmax over the purchased product's category minus whatever else the basket holds from that category. Its choice set averages 46.9 admissible products, so chance is −log 46.9 = −3.848 and 2.1% top-1.

Not comparable to earlier numbers

Every item log-likelihood previously reported for this project was a 21-way or 201-way discrimination against sampled decoys. Those metrics were tilted by the proposal distribution — their optimal scorer is log p − log q, not log p. Nothing here can be placed alongside them; only the distance from chance carries across.

The seed spread is 0.0095 nats (two seeds, identical but for initialisation). That is a weak handle on refitting variability, so gaps are now also bootstrapped.

Confidence intervals — household block bootstrap

Each basket is scored once per model, then households are resampled with replacement (400 draws, 1,664 households, 6,000 held-out baskets). Households are the unit because trips repeat within a household and are not independent. Gaps are paired: the same resampled households score both models, so the interval is on the difference rather than the difference of two intervals.

What these intervals do not cover

Evaluation uncertainty only. Refitting variability — optimisation noise, initialisation, and the training sample — is not included, because a full bootstrap would need a refit per replicate at ~25 minutes each. The two-seed spread of 0.0095 nats is the only handle on that, and it is of the same order as the smallest gaps quoted. A gap of +0.056 comfortably exceeds both sources; a gap of 0.02 would not.

2Do the equations match the code?

Fifteen checks compare each printed formula against the fitted model, by autograd where the specification states a derivative and by direct evaluation where it states an identity (scripts/33_verify_equations.py). All fifteen pass, including the three that carry the argument: the swap identity E(S′) − E(S) = u−jm − u−jj, the unbiasedness of the sampled inclusive value, and the three price derivatives of Appendix C.

Unequal tuning budget

The heuristics have one tuned weight each. The fitted model has 870,392 parameters and its architecture was chosen across many configurations. This comparison is not like-for-like and should not be read as one; the learned baselines that would make it fair (HPF, B-Emb) have not been refitted against the current specification.

What this does and does not show

Permuting a product's price series across days destroys the price–demand association whether it is causal or confounded. The test rules out a spurious constant. It cannot distinguish "price moves demand" from "the retailer times promotions to demand", which is the actual endogeneity concern for within-product price variation. There is no instrument here and no discontinuity. The section title in the earlier paper — "Is the price response causal?" — overstated what this design answers.

Co-occurrence

Spearman rank correlation of pairwise lift against real: +0.028 at 0 sweeps, +0.082 at 4. The previous session reached +0.097 only by drawing 75% of negatives from the true product's own category. That mechanism no longer exists — there are no negatives — and §6 of the specification predicted category normalisation would absorb most of the gain. It reached +0.082 of +0.097, so the prediction is partly confirmed: most of the effect was the choice-set mismatch, not negative difficulty, but not all of it.

Negative result — fine-grained substitution

Within-basket pairs sharing a held-out sub-commodity label: real 0.0646, generated 0.0295 at 0 sweeps and 0.0375 at 4. The generator under-produces fine substitution by about 40%, and Gibbs sweeps close only part of it. At department level it is nearly right (real 0.5581, generated 0.5399), so the failure is specific to fine distinctions. This is the weakest result in the study.

The resolution: α is organised where the data is, and nowhere else

Restricting to the 23,892 product pairs that actually co-occur at least 8 times in 40,000 baskets, cosine similarity separates same-sub-commodity pairs at AUC 0.9172, with mean cosine +0.552 for same-sub pairs against +0.118 for different-sub. It also predicts top-quartile co-purchase lift at AUC 0.8253, and correlates with lift at Spearman +0.596 — still +0.508 among different-sub-commodity pairs, so it is not merely re-encoding the taxonomy.

The weak all-pairs AUC is therefore dominated by pairs the model has no co-purchase evidence about. Splitting by training volume confirms it: same-sub AUC rises 0.60 → 0.70 and the cosine gap widens from 0.092/0.007 to 0.227/0.045 as products go from 50–150 training lines to 400–1,200.

Nearest-neighbour inspection is a useful corrective to the summary statistics. Among frequently bought products: CORN YELLOW → CORN WHITE (0.62) is right, but BANANAS → VANILLA WAFER/KIDS COOKIES (0.53), FLUID MILK → DESIGNER PAPER (0.40) and EGGS → CELERY (0.52) are not taxonomy at all. They are basket relationships. α is doing what Eq. 11 asks — making co-purchased products similar — and taxonomy agreement is a by-product of the fact that similar things are bought together.

Negative result — household embeddings carry no demographic signal

Not one probe beats always predicting the majority class. θ scores at the shuffled-label level on income (0.152 against 0.152). γ reaches exactly the majority rate on four of five targets, which is what a probe does when it finds nothing and defaults to the base class. Concatenating the blocks makes it worse, the signature of overfitting: 136 features against 655 households.

The honest reading is that the probe is linear and the sample is small, so this rules out a linear, low-sample relationship rather than any relationship. But it is the natural first test, and it fails. The household parameters are fitted from purchases and appear to encode purchase behaviour only — not who the household is.

This ordering is economically sensible

Cigarettes and baby food are the textbook inelastic goods — addiction and non-negotiable need. Bath tissue, cold cereal and bacon are storable, heavily promoted and brand-switchable, which is exactly where a shopper can respond to a price move. The model was given no category labels and no elasticity priors; this ordering is a consequence of the fit, and it is the strongest evidence in this document that γ⊤β is estimating something real.

Two negative findings about gij

24.28% of (household, product) pairs have the wrong sign — the model says those households buy more when the product is dearer. Nothing in the specification constrains γ⊤β to be positive (§18 of the specification lists this), so upward-sloping demand is representable and the fit uses it for a quarter of all pairs. Aggregate elasticities remain negative, but no individual (household, product) elasticity should be quoted.

Household price sensitivity is unrelated to income: Spearman +0.0046, p = 0.906, n = 655. The expected economic pattern — poorer households more price sensitive — is entirely absent. Combined with §7, the household side of the price interaction has no demonstrated external validity.

The choice margin and the quantity margin are fitted separately and agree only weakly: Spearman +0.138 across products. Nothing imposes agreement, so this is a measurement rather than a check — but it means "price-sensitive product" means something different at the two margins.

9Could this target coupons?

Targeting has value only if the model's predicted heterogeneity matches real heterogeneity out of sample. Households were ranked by predicted gij using train-fitted parameters, then their actual response to price deviations was measured on held-out weeks: the within-pair slope of the purchase indicator on Δlog p, over the trips that household made.

Targeting would work, but coarsely

The top two quintiles average a real slope of −0.347 against −0.016 for the bottom two — a 22× difference in actual responsiveness. Q1, the households the model says are insensitive, show a real slope of +0.0013: no measurable response at all. So sending coupons to the predicted-top 40% rather than at random does buy a large difference in who responds.

But the individual-level signal is weak, and not monotone

Spearman between predicted g and real slope is −0.1252 (p = 6.4×10⁻⁵⁴, n = 15,138). Highly significant, but |ρ| ≈ 0.13 means the ranking is right on average and unreliable for any individual pair. Q4 is more responsive than Q5 (−0.3639 vs −0.3297), so the ordering breaks at the top. And the correlation is no better aggregated — products −0.156, households −0.165 — so the weakness is not averaged-out noise but a genuine ceiling.

Practical reading: usable for a coarse split (target the top two quintiles), not for per-household coupon values or a bidding system. Combined with §7 and §8 — household embeddings carry no demographic signal, sensitivity is 46% a product property against 17% a household one, and 24% of pairs have the wrong sign — the household dimension of gij is the weakest part of the model, and coupon targeting leans on precisely that dimension.

10Can generated baskets train a Markovian policy?

Correction — this section previously reported the opposite

An earlier version concluded "no", on the basis that 46% of generated purchases were products the household had never bought against 14% real. That number was an artefact of a bug in the measurement, not a property of the model. generate_baskets() selects a random subset of test baskets and did not return which ones; the analysis attributed the k-th generated basket to test basket k, so every "has this household bought this before?" question was asked against a different household's history. Two random households share about 15% of their products, which is exactly the repeat rate that kept appearing. The function now returns its trip indices so the mistake cannot recur.

A pricing policy learned on synthetic baskets needs the generator to behave as a Markov decision process. This model has one dynamic state — the purchase-recency vector xijt — so the question is whether generation reproduces its distribution. Measured correctly, matching each generated basket to its own household:

The generator over-produces novelty by roughly 20%, not the 240% the buggy measurement implied. Two model changes account for most of the improvement: separating the incidence penalty (§11) closed 64% of the novel-category gap, and a per-(household, product) persistence term closed 55% of the novel-item gap. Together they close 81% and 64% of the two gaps respectively.

Revised verdict — closer to usable, still not established

An 18.93% never-bought rate against a real 13.89% is a materially different situation from 46% against 14%: the state distribution is in the right region rather than in a different one. That removes the categorical objection to multi-step rollouts, but does not establish that they are sound. A 36% relative excess of novelty still compounds over a horizon, and the three structural obstructions below are untouched by any of this.

  • Prices are exogenous. The model conditions on Δlog p and never generates it, so there is no feedback from a policy's action to future prices.
  • Δlog p is centred on the training window. A policy that shifts the price level moves products outside the support the coefficients were fitted on.
  • No budget constraint. Nothing couples spending across categories, so a policy cutting prices everywhere produces unbounded purchasing rather than substitution.

What can be said: single-step counterfactuals of the kind in §5 are supported. Multi-step rollouts are no longer ruled out by the state distribution, but would need the residual novelty excess quantified over a horizon before being trusted.

11Three limitations, attacked

The assortment definition — worse than a selection problem

𝒞s(c) is "sold at least once in training", so the choice set is built from the choices. Refitting under other rules is expensive, so instead the choice set was rebuilt at evaluation time under stricter thresholds with the fitted model held fixed.

The estimand is not invariant to an unjustified threshold

The allocation elasticity runs from −0.61 to 0.00 as the threshold moves from 1 to 25 sales. Part of that is mechanical and should be said plainly: the elasticity is −(γ⊤β)(1 − πj), and shrinking the choice set drives πj → 1, so the factor (1 − πj) → 0 by construction. This test therefore does not isolate outcome selection from that mechanical dependence.

But it establishes something arguably worse than the original concern: the headline elasticity depends on a modelling choice nobody has justified. "Sold at least once" was picked because a threshold of three deleted genuine purchases; no argument was ever given that it is the right definition of what a shopper could have bought. Any elasticity quoted from this model is conditional on that choice, and the sensitivity is first-order, not second.

Novelty — not a sharpness problem

The natural hypothesis for 46% novelty against 14% real is that the item conditional is too flat, so sampling produces variety a real shopper does not show. A single temperature on the generation softmax should then fix it.

The hypothesis is wrong. Sharpening by a factor of four moves novelty only 46.1% → 43.5%, nowhere near the real 14.2%, while basket size inflates 4.6×. Median staleness of repeat purchases stays at 80–91 days against a real 32 at every temperature. Whatever produces the novelty is not the sharpness of the item softmax.

This test is confounded, and the confound is instructive

Scaling the item utility also scales the inclusive value, which feeds incidence — so basket size moves for a reason that has nothing to do with the item conditional. The temperature column is therefore not a clean intervention on item choice alone. What survives the confound is the flat novelty rate: it sits at 42–46% across a 4× range of sharpness and a 4.6× range of basket size, so it is robust to both. A clean test would scale only inside the item softmax and leave IV alone.

A joint test at last

Every likelihood in this study is a conditional, and §6 compares summary statistics one at a time, which cannot see a joint mismatch. A classifier two-sample test can: train a discriminator to separate real baskets from generated ones on basket-level features. AUC 0.5 means indistinguishable.

12What is still not established

  1. Refitting uncertainty is still missing. §1 now carries a household block bootstrap, but it covers evaluation sampling only. A full bootstrap needs a refit per replicate and has not been run.
  2. The test set has been used repeatedly. Eight models are compared on it here, on top of the iteration that produced the specification. Nothing reserves it for a single final evaluation.
  3. Learned baselines are stale. HPF and B-Emb were fitted against the old sampled-softmax code and have not been refitted, so §4 compares against heuristics only.
  4. Identification is unaddressed. §18 of the specification lists a 2,016-dimensional rotational invariance in α and θ, an unconstrained sign on γ⊤β — now measured at 24.28% negative — and the fact that the pseudo-likelihood never compares across categories, so category levels are identified only through the noisy inclusive value. None of this is tested here.
  5. The assortment threshold is unjustified and the estimand is sensitive to it (§11). Not resolved — resolving it needs an assortment measure independent of purchases, which this data does not contain.
  6. Multi-step generation is not established (§10). The residual novelty excess is ~20% relative, down from an apparent 240% that was a measurement artefact; its effect over a horizon has not been quantified.
  7. The joint is now tested and it fails (§11, AUC 0.8101). The price-response check in §6 remains circular: it re-estimates a quantity the model was fitted to, so it verifies the sampler propagates the coefficient, not that the coefficient is right.
